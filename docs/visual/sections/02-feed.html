02. The narratives feed

The home screen. A single list of the stories going viral on the internet right now, best one at the top, each one explained in two lines of plain English so you never have to watch the video to know what it is.

Every other tool a trader opens — Axiom, Photon, GMGN — is a list of coins. Insidor's home screen is a list of stories, and most of them do not have a coin yet. That is the whole point. If a row already has money flowing into it, you were late. The feed's job is to put the ferry captain in front of you at 09:13, eighteen minutes before anybody turns it into a coin called $FERRY and thirty-four minutes before crypto Twitter notices.

The order of the list is the product. Behind it sits everything described in section 01 — how fast views are climbing, how many separate people are posting the same thing, whether the AI thinks a coin could plausibly be made out of it. None of that appears on screen. The user gets the order; the order is the answer. Putting our numbers next to the rows would hand the judgement back to the person we are meant to be making it for.

The two lines are the product

The most important text in the whole app is the sentence under each title. "A retiring ferry captain blew his horn one last time, and every boat in the harbour blew back." Read that and you already know whether it is worth a coin — you can feel whether it is funny, whether it has a name in it, whether people will repeat it. So it is set larger and brighter than the title above it. It is not a subtitle. It is the row.

Everything else on the row is a fact about the world that would be true whether or not Insidor existed: how many people have seen it, whether the line is still going up, how old it is, and what you can do about it. Seven things, no eighth.

The seven elements, and why each one earns its place

1
A picture from the post

The still frame from the video or image. It is how you recognise a story you have already seen scrolling elsewhere, in a fifth of a second, before reading anything.

2
A title

"Harbor Last Horn." A short handle so the story can be referred to, searched for, and pasted into a group chat. Deliberately set smaller than the sentence below it.

3
Two lines of plain English

The point of the entire screen. You should understand the story without watching the video. Largest, brightest text in the row — it is what you actually judge the trade on.

4
How many views

The headline number, big and unambiguous. A true fact about the world: this many people have seen it. Not our estimate of anything, just the count.

5
A tiny line graph

The view count over the last hour. Answers the one question a single number cannot: is it still going up, or is it over? Cyan and rising, or grey and flat.

6
How old it is

Four hours. Six hours. Combined with the graph this is the whole timing story — 850K in two hours is a different animal from 850K in two days.

7
One button

Create coin if nobody has made one, Buy $TICKER if somebody has, 3 coins → if several exist and you need to pick. One row, one obvious next move.

What we removed, and where it went

An earlier version of this screen showed the row as a trading terminal: columns headed HEAT, ACCEL, ORG and MEME, a twenty-minute chart of view-rate change, and a footer counting how many posts our filters had thrown away. Every number on it was real. It still had to go, because it asks the user to do the job we built the company to do for them.

SAME STORY, SAME SECOND, SAME UNDERLYING DATA. THE TOP ROW ASKS THE READER TO WEIGH UP SIX NUMBERS AND DECIDE. THE BOTTOM ROW HAS ALREADY DECIDED, AND SAYS SO BY SITTING AT THE TOP OF THE LIST. NOTE THAT VIEWS, AGE AND THE ACTION SURVIVE THE CUT — THOSE ARE FACTS, NOT OPINIONS.

To be completely clear: we still calculate every one of those numbers. They are what decides the order of the feed, which stories get grouped together, and which of the hundreds of millions of daily posts we bother looking at twice. They are written into the Library on every cycle and they are what section 07 will eventually learn from. They simply never leave the building.
