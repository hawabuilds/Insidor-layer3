08. The algorithm — how we find viral posts

There is no feed of everything. The detector is a budget-allocation problem wearing a statistics costume: we can afford to look at roughly one post in a thousand, and the product is the discipline of choosing which thousandth.

1. The constraint

X publishes on the order of half a billion posts a day. Nobody reads them all and no vendor sells them all. Our reseller bills about $0.00015 per post returned, so a defensible $75/day sensor budget buys roughly 500,000 reads — one per thousand posts published. Worse than it sounds, because a read is not a measurement: to know something is growing you must read it twice, which puts the ceiling on distinct measurable posts nearer one in five thousand.

So the first design decision is not a model, it is a filter that costs nothing. Six arithmetic rules over the arriving payload — roster, media, length, named entity, minus bait regex, minus an image seen in the last 30 days — drop ~70% before a single paid call.

THE FUNNEL — one day, X only · widths illustrative, ratios exact

The top strip is a day of X drawn to scale; the cyan hairline is the whole budget. The expensive part is not the top of the funnel but the middle, where 30,000 posts each need a second look before any of them can be ranked.

2. Why the obvious approach fails

The obvious detector is a saved search — min_faves:100, polled, alert on what returns. One line of code, and what most competitors run. It cannot work for us for an arithmetic reason: a post does not cross 100 likes until it has been travelling twenty to sixty minutes, by which point the coin is frequently already minted. A threshold search does not find viral posts early. It finds posts that have already gone viral and sells you the receipt.

TIMELINE — median case · minutes since the post was published

Same post, two detectors. Ours decides at minute 9 and shows at minute 12. A 100-like search cannot resolve the post before minute 20 and often not before minute 60 — the wrong side of the mint. The product lives in that gap.

3. What we do instead — three lanes

Rather than raising the engagement bar to cut volume, we drop it to the floor and cut volume with the free arrival prior instead. A min_faves:15 net over a rolling six-minute window, re-run every three minutes, is account-agnostic. That matters because no roster covers the case we most need: a brand-new account, or a dev wallet's first post. The net removes ~99% of raw volume while keeping essentially everything that could matter. Beside it sit a curated push stream on ~500 known amplifiers — the only path that delivers in seconds — and TikTok topic surfaces, because TikTok has no firehose and must be polled by subject.

THREE LANES — different jobs, one admit gate

Lane B is the one people underrate: cheapest of the three, and the only one that can see an account we have never heard of — precisely the account that posts the clip a coin gets minted from.

4. The measurement — a geometric snapshot grid

Once admitted, a post is re-read on a fixed, widening schedule: 4, 9, 14, 21, 30, 42, 58 and 78 minutes, each gap about 1.5× the last. Early on everything moves and observations are worth a lot; an hour in the trajectory is set and dense sampling is money burnt.

This is also where the earliest-possible alert comes from, and it is worth being blunt: one observation is a level, not a rate. A post at 900 views at minute 4 might be decelerating from 800 views/min or accelerating from 40 — you cannot tell, at any price, with any model. The first honest rate exists at τ=9, and no infrastructure spend moves that floor. It is physics, not engineering. Everything the roadmap can improve sits after minute 9.

SNAPSHOT GRID — τ, minutes since publish · ratio ≈ 1.5

Nothing before the cyan tick is a signal, however big the number looks. Designing the alert around this prevents the classic failure: firing on one large reading that turns out to be the tail of something which peaked before we arrived.

5. The signal — rate relative to the author, then acceleration

Raw numbers are nearly meaningless. 10,000 views in ten minutes is a thunderclap for a 512-follower ferry-spotting account and a quiet Tuesday for one with 2M followers. So every rate is scored against that author's own recent baseline. A thin-history account gets a wide band borrowed from its follower bucket, which stops us mistaking ignorance for signal. Then we take the second derivative: two posts can sit at identical levels while one is compounding and the other has turned over, and acceleration is the only thing that separates heating from already peaked.

Left: why a small account can outrank a huge one. Right: why the board ranks on acceleration rather than size — the amber curve is the "found it after it was over" failure, and level alone cannot detect it.

6. Is any of this machine learning?

Mostly no, and we should say so plainly rather than let anyone assume a trained model exists in here that does not. Detection and ranking are classical statistics: rates, baselines, quantiles. Judgement calls that need language understanding go to Claude. Similarity uses an off-the-shelf embedding model exactly as sold. Exactly one model is fit by us — a logistic regression that turns a matching score into a calibrated probability. No training runs, no GPUs, no labelled corpus beyond a few hundred hand-checked pairs.

7. Proving we were early

A lead-time number is trivially faked by measuring it backwards. Alert first, then go looking for the coin, and we choose which coin counts and when the clock started — lookahead bias, and it turns the headline number into a lie. So both reference clocks run ahead of the pipeline, independently of it: a websocket records every Solana mint as it happens, a poll over ~300 crypto-Twitter accounts records every ticker mention, and both indexes are written before any alert fires. promoted_at is stamped once and never overwritten; lead time is the distance to the first entry either clock already holds.

TWO PROSPECTIVE CLOCKS — both written before the alert

Both tapes are recording continuously whether or not we ever alert. That is what makes the lead-time figure an observation rather than a claim — and it is why we can publish the misses alongside it.

One consequence the team should internalise. A post first seen when it is already large is permanently marked ineligible for the earliness statistic. It can still appear on the board under an "Already Hot" label, but it never counts toward lead time. Any other choice quietly inflates the one number the product is sold on.
