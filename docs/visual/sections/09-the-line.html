09. Where the line sits

Part three is where the two halves of this document meet. Everything before this was the machine; everything after is the screen. Exactly one rule governs what is allowed to cross between them.

The rule is short. Facts about the world go on screen. Our reasoning never does. A view count is a fact — the post really has 2.4 million views whether or not Insidor was ever built. A market cap is a fact. So is the time a post went up, the name of the account that posted it, and how many people hold a coin. All of those existed before we looked and would carry on existing if we shut down tomorrow.

Our reasoning is a different kind of thing. When our system decides a story is worth showing, it is doing arithmetic: comparing one view count against the same post's view count twenty minutes earlier, weighing how many separate people posted about the same ferry captain, asking the AI how memeable the thing is and getting a number back. Those numbers are real and they matter enormously — they are how the ordering is decided — but they exist only because we invented them. They are our workings, and workings do not belong in front of a user.

In the founder's words: "The app's job is to make the judgement so the user doesn't have to. Showing our workings would just make them do our job for us." That is the whole argument. A trader opening Insidor at 09:13 has perhaps ninety seconds before the window closes. If we hand them a heat score of 8.4 and an acceleration ratio of 3.1x, we have not helped them decide — we have handed them a second decision on top of the first one, and asked them to learn our internal scale before they can use the product. The correct output of all that arithmetic is not a number. It is an ordered list and one plain sentence.

The two-liner is not a summary of the reasoning, it is the reasoning, delivered.

The test

Any new screen, any new field, any argument about whether something belongs — this settles it in about five seconds, and anyone on the team can apply it without asking.

Before you put a number on screen, ask
Would this number still be true if Insidor did not exist?
Yes → it can be shown
2.4M views — the post has them either way
$799.6K — on-chain, public, checkable
4h old — the clock is not ours
1,204 holders — anyone can count them
No → it stays behind the scenes
meme_score 7 — we invented the scale
accel 3.1x — needs our own history to exist
match 87% — our uncertainty, not theirs
rank ▲4 — a fact about our list, not the world

One question, applied to every field. It has a useful side effect: the numbers that pass are exactly the numbers a sceptical user can verify elsewhere, so showing them builds credibility. The numbers that fail are the ones we could never prove anyway.
