06. The six parts of the machine

Everything behind the app is six moving parts. Four of them run on their own, on a schedule, with nobody watching. One is a database in the middle. The last one is the app itself. This section is the part of Insidor the user never sees.

The job the machine has to do is narrow: find a post that is going viral right now, work out whether anyone could make a memecoin out of it, and notice when lots of separate posts are all about the same thing. Everything else — the prices, the charts, the wallet, the buying — is rented from companies who already do it well. So the machine is small on purpose. Six parts, and only two of them are things nobody else can do for us.

They pass work along a line. The Collector brings in candidate posts. The Measurer watches those posts over time. The Judge reads the ones that are moving fastest. The Grouper bundles related ones together into a single story. All four write what they find into the Library, which is one database and the single source of truth. The App only ever reads out of the Library. It never talks to X, or to Claude, or to the data reseller directly.

How work flows — four writers, one Library, one reader

Work moves left to right along the top, and everything drains down into one database. The App sits underneath and only reads. If a job dies overnight, the Library still has everything it wrote before it died, and the App carries on serving it.

1 · The Collector

Runs every couple of minutes. It asks a data reseller — a company that already has a firehose of social posts and sells access to it — for posts that are getting a lot of attention right now. It cannot look at everything: hundreds of millions of posts are made every day, and paying to read all of them would cost more than the company is worth. So it filters hard before anything else happens, and only a small shortlist survives to the next step.

2 · The Measurer — the part that is easy to skip and impossible to replace

The Measurer takes every post the Collector shortlisted and looks at it again a few minutes later. And again. And again, for the first several hours of its life. Each time, it writes down one thing: how many views the post has, and the exact minute it checked.

This sounds trivial and it is the whole product. Here is why. A single view count tells you almost nothing. Five hundred thousand views could mean the post exploded in ten minutes — in which case it is happening right now and you want in — or that it crawled to the same number over two days, in which case it is finished and everyone who cared already saw it. The number on the post is identical in both cases. Only by measuring the same post twice can you tell the difference.

Two posts, the same 500,000 views, opposite meanings

Both posts hit exactly 500,000. A tool that reads the number once cannot tell them apart. The difference only exists in the gap between two readings — and that gap is what the Measurer is manufacturing, five minutes at a time.

Why this is urgent

This history cannot be bought later. Nobody sells "what did this post look like twenty minutes ago."

You can buy today's view count from a dozen places. You cannot buy the shape of how it got there, because nobody was writing it down. If we are not recording it as it happens, it is gone forever — and every day we run, the record gets longer and a competitor starting tomorrow starts with none of it.

3 · The Judge

Claude reads the posts that are climbing fastest and answers one question: could someone realistically make a memecoin out of this? Most viral posts are useless to us. Politics, sport, a plane crash, a news bulletin — huge numbers, nothing to name a coin after. What works is one absurd thing that has a name: a frog called Gerald, a plastic plant, a ferry horn. When the answer is yes, the Judge also proposes a coin name and a ticker. Understanding humour is exactly the sort of thing a language model is for, and exactly the sort of thing a rule you write by hand will never do.

4 · The Grouper

One person posting a video of a Norwegian ferry captain is luck. Twenty people independently posting about the same captain, on two different platforms, within an hour, is a meme actually forming. The Grouper spots that ten separate posts are about the same thing and bundles them into one narrative — which is the unit the whole app is built around. Without it the feed is ten near-identical rows about a ferry. With it, there is one story with ten pieces of evidence behind it.

5 · The Library, and 6 · The App

The Library is one database. Everything the four jobs learn goes in there and nowhere else, which means there is never a question of which copy is right. The App reads narratives out of it and puts them on screen. That is the only direction data moves: the App never reaches out to a social network or an AI itself, so a slow reseller or a failed AI call can never make the page hang.

Where all of this lives

The first four are not something anyone runs. They are scheduled jobs on a server: they fire on a timetable, whether or not our laptops are open, whether or not anyone is using the app. Nobody presses anything. If one fails it retries on its next turn, and the Library keeps whatever it already wrote.

Two-minute checks are the floor, not the ceiling of ambition — a post that goes from 41,000 to 118,000 views between two checks is invisible to anyone sampling once an hour. The cost of running all of this is roughly £5 a day, with a hard cap that pauses collection rather than quietly running up a bill.
