The one rule to carry through every screen

The primary button is never a copywriting choice. It is a function of how many confirmed coins a story has: none means Create, one means Buy, several means Compare. When the system is not certain a coin belongs to a story, the candidates are still shown — labelled unverified — and the Buy button is switched off. A wrong Buy button on a memecoin terminal is a user losing money, so that state is made unrepresentable rather than merely discouraged.

01. Live Feed — the posts lane

A ranked board of the forty posts going viral right now, ordered by how fast each is accelerating against its own author's baseline — not by how many views it has. One job: what is blowing up, and how much head start is left.

This is deliberately the pre-coin surface. Most rows have no ticker and nothing to buy — not a gap in the data but the product's whole claim, since a row with a liquid coin attached means you were late. Inside Live feed a switch splits POSTS, STORIES and COINS, which share one row height, one header and one stability model, so the three read as views of a single instrument.

Below sits a 44px control strip: platform chips (rendered from select distinct platform, so X and TikTok and nothing else — three hardcoded chips reading zero forever would be a lie), age window, sort chips, thresholds, and one loud red SHOW RISKY. That toggle is the only control that relaxes a hard eligibility gate, which is why it is red, in the open rather than a drawer, and never persisted. On the right the strip reports on itself — LAST TICK 4s — because a board that reranks silently looks identical to one that has died.

Why REACH is grey and HEAT is white

REACH is the one number here we refuse to render in primary ink. Cumulative views are an eligibility gate, not the score — a post clears a floor to be on the board at all, and after that its total says almost nothing about whether you still have a head start. Every competing surface trains users to read the biggest number in a row as the ranking. Render 2.4M in white beside a HEAT of 94 and the eye lands on the reach, re-sorting the board in the trader's head. Muting it costs nothing and kills the likeliest misread of this screen. HEAT takes the white number and the cyan bar because HEAT is the sort key made legible.

Why ACCEL shows an em-dash

ACCEL turns about forty seconds before the rank does, which is what a trader actually watches. It is a ratio of two exponentially-weighted view-rate accumulators, so it needs two snapshots to exist. On a post seen exactly once the honest value is not computable, and the obvious placeholder, 1.0×, is the worst available lie: it reads as steady state, sits in the amber band, and is indistinguishable from a real measurement of a post that is genuinely flat. So one snapshot renders a dim —, a blank sparkline and an asterisked HEAT. Same rule under an ingest gap: the accumulator freezes rather than decays, and the window is marked unmeasurable instead of scored as a win.

There is not a single like button on this screen

Nothing inside the POST cell is an engagement control. No like, no repost, no reply, no follow — and this is not an omission we plan to fill. Insidor is not a client for X or TikTok and must never look like one: the moment a row offers a repost affordance the board becomes a feed, the mental model becomes consumption, and every honesty label reads as decoration around content rather than a fact about an instrument. Name, handle and timestamp link out to the real post; that is where you engage. Create coin and Buy are Insidor actions on an Insidor object, so they sit outside the post cell behind a 1px divider separating what the internet made from what we are offering you.

The action is a pure function of one number: how many coins on this post's story are matched confirmed and clear the hard safety gates. Zero gives Create coin, one gives Buy $TICKER, two or more gives See the N coins. That count is market-data independent — a coin confirmed four minutes ago with a null mcap is still one, still Buy. Demanding a non-null mcap first would flip that mint back to Create and drive a duplicate launch in the exact window this product exists to serve.

02. Live Feed — the coins lane

The same board, switched to the tradeable side: what is moving right now, and — in the slot every competitor fills with a decorative sparkline — where it came from. One job: turn a story into the specific mint you can buy, without ever showing a number we do not have.

The posts lane answers what is blowing up; the coins lane answers what can I do about it. Same instrument — same 54px rows, hairlines, rank column, 20-second commit. The one real difference is the STORY column, and that column is the entire company. DexScreener, Photon, Axiom and Pump.fun render the same eleven market columns off the same three vendors; none can tell you this mint is the ferry captain's last horn, that we saw the post 34 minutes before crypto Twitter did, and that the trace is auditable at a URL. So it sits in slot three, right after the ticker, and it is the last column the responsive rules may drop.

The board is never one list. Four bands — Fresh (on the bonding curve), Graduating, Live (migrated, has a pool), From a Story (one row per narrative) — each with its own column template and sort key. A three-minute $8K curve token and a $40M AMM token are not comparable quantities, and a table showing both draws four structural dashes per row across a whole band: a table admitting it was built for the wrong data.

How a 31-minute-old token ranks against a three-day-old one

Four mechanisms, none a hand-tuned weight. Band partition: a curve token lives in Fresh and cannot enter the same ordered list as a migrated one. Market cap and liquidity are never terms — display columns and hard gates only, which is why $MOTH's fifteen-fold size advantage buys it nothing. Acceleration is self-normalised: five-minute volume against the coin's own last hour, so a coin is only compared to itself and being big earns zero. And in Fresh an age penalty (1.87× at twenty minutes, 7.94× at three hours) turns the band over completely in about forty minutes. One more is the thesis in the formula: a story-linked coin is multiplied by 0.35 the moment crypto Twitter picks the story up — when our edge is gone the coin falls off the board while it is still climbing.

Note what story attachment does not do: it never multiplies rank in the coin-subject bands, only filters and breaks ties. If it multiplied, the incentive would be to game the matcher — the precision-critical component in the system — so keeping story off the multiplier keeps the attack surface off the correctness-critical path.

The RISK chip: why there are no green ticks

Every competitor's safety panel opens with two permanent green ticks — mint authority revoked, freeze authority revoked. The pump.fun program revokes both at creation, so every token passes. A check the whole population passes carries zero bits, and worse, it teaches the user that green means safe in a panel where green means nothing; after a week of two ticks on every rug they stop reading it. Neither is rendered as a signal; both appear once in the popover footer as a dim non-signal line saying why.

What replaces them: worst discriminating signal on the left, fixed obtainability denominator on the right — T10 78 · 4/9. Nine signals would discriminate (dev holding, sniper, bundle and insider percentages, creator history, top-10 concentration, single-holder maximum, holder count, sell-side liveness); we can compute four today, and five need first-slot transaction analysis we are not building yet. The denominator is always visible because a missing chip reads as "fine", and a lime tick is unreachable until creator history has a verdict for that wallet — which is why $PIGEON reads ? 4/9.

03. The Story page — the judge screen

/story/[id] is where a user settles two questions — "is this real?" and "am I early or late?" — and then takes exactly one action. Everything else in the product is a way of getting someone to this page.

It is a routed page, not a modal, and that is a product decision rather than a routing preference. The whole pitch is a lead-time claim, and a claim nobody can open, link and check is marketing. A URL a stranger can paste into Discord and verify against two public timestamps is the only version of this that survives a sceptic.

Two columns. Left is evidence, read top to bottom as one document. Right is the persistent rail, which on this route opens on a fourth mode — Discussion — beside the tape modes it carries everywhere else. The rail does not disappear here: a trader sits on this page longer than any other, and no terminal hides the tape on its analysis view.

The full page in its most common state: one confirmed coin, a positive lead time, a live pipeline. Notice that LEAD TIME and YOUR CLOCK are different cells in different colours. +34m is a historical fact about the detector, frozen at promote; promoted 4h00m ago · 1 coin since · you are not early is the live question the person on the page actually has. They never share a component, and the sticky action bar never renders +34m — a frozen number beside a Buy button reads as a promise about the trade.

The evidence column is arranged so a sceptic runs out of objections in order. The source post carries the real permalink from platform_post_id; if that id is missing, no link renders and a footnote says so. We never synthesise one — a dead link on the proof surface costs more than a missing one. The virality grid puts a 30-minute delta on every cell, so a large number that stopped growing is visibly distinct from a smaller one still climbing.

The momentum chart puts posts-per-5-minute bars behind the views area on purpose: views can keep climbing while the rate of new posts collapses, and that divergence is the acceleration signal the promote trigger uses. The series is a written rollup table, not a view over raw snapshots — posts are sampled on a geometric grid, so naive summing makes totals drop whenever fewer posts happened to snapshot, and COOLING would fire on a sampling artefact. The lifecycle word gets one coloured bar and a mono readout, not a paragraph: the page keeps exactly two sentences of English — the lead-time claim and the outcome verdict — because both are falsifiable assertions and should read as such.

The stamp: earliness, made visible and permanent for people

Every comment carries a line frozen at insert: posted when 340K views · no coin yet · 40m old · early. Lead time makes the product's earliness checkable; the stamp does the same job for a person. Someone who called the ferry story at 340K views, before any coin existed, has a permanent public receipt — and someone who arrived at 3.1M views has one too, in exactly the same slot, unflattering and uneditable.

Two constraints make it real. Every snap column, including snap_early, is written once and guarded by a BEFORE UPDATE trigger — computing "early" at render against live views would let the badge drift monotonically in the author's favour as the story grows. And Earliest sorts snap_views ASC, not created_at ASC: earliest means written when the story was smallest, which is the thing worth ranking. Votes are farmable; a frozen snapshot of the world at write time is not.

The position badge is the other half. It is read once server-side at write time and stored as a raw amount with decimals and mint, so it renders holds 2.1M $FERRY, no position or sold beside the handle. No other crypto comment section can render this, because no other one owns both the story and the trade: Twitter knows the take and not the wallet, a DEX knows the wallet and not the take. It answers the question that matters when you read a bullish comment — is this person talking their book — before you read the sentence. The denomination is the token, never SOL: native SOL is unrelated to the coin, and SOL-denominating a position needs a price quote we do not fetch. A failed chain read renders position unknown, dashed, never no position — which would be a fabricated credential in the author's favour.

The coin section: one panel shell, four states

The panel occupies the same shell in every state, above the fold, primary always bottom-right at 40px and 168px minimum width. Only the label moves. A count chip carries the state's signature so a zero never reads as an error. One pure, unit-tested resolver produces the label from verdict, mint_time and ticker; unsure.length is in its return value and nowhere in its branch condition.

The funding cliff, stated honestly

For a user arriving from a TikTok link with no SOL, the path to holding $FERRY is not one flow but two: card → SOL through the onramp, then SOL → FERRY through the swap. That is roughly 2.5% on the onramp leg plus 0.60% on the swap, and it takes long enough that the price on the second leg is not the price they saw on the first. This is the single largest drop-off in the product and we should not pretend otherwise. Direct-to-mint fiat funding is cut from v1 because it is unverified that the onramp can deliver an arbitrary SPL mint, and shipping on that assumption makes the normie path fiction. We ship honest arithmetic instead: both legs and both fees stated end to end before the user starts, the shortfall prefilled, and the drift on return shown at equal weight behind an explicit Buy anyway.

How the auto-namer works

A memecoin's name is not authored, it is retrieved. WIF keeps the misspelling because the misspelling is the meme. So this is retrieval over the whole cluster — every post, its OCR'd on-screen text, the sampled replies — and it is ours to do because we hold the cluster and pump.fun holds one form.

A deterministic harvester runs at promote time: no model, four milliseconds. Six weighted sources — cashtag 1.00, on-screen text 0.85 (on TikTok the caption is the meme more often than the audio), repeated n-grams 0.80, entities 0.75, the classifier's own suggestion last at 0.35 and never primary without corroboration. Scoring rewards a span frequent inside this story and rare across the last 24 hours of everything, penalises length outside four to nine characters, and penalises symbols already carrying deep liquidity.

One Haiku call fires on form open and does two things: rank the harvested spans, title-case them. It does not invent. A server gate checks that the span literally appears in the post text, OCR or replies, that the symbol is that span or a whole-word subset of it, plus length and a blocklist. Any failure discards the entire model output and the deterministic ranking stands. The gate also runs on a ticker prefilled from a search row — clicking is not evidence.

Normalisation drops stopwords first: PAIDTOMOVE survives from "paid to move to Norway". Still too long and we drop the least distinctive whole word, then fall back to initials. We never truncate mid-word — $PAIDTOMOV signals low effort to every trader who sees it.

The form never waits on the model: it opens in under 100ms on the deterministic candidate with the trust line shimmering, and the model result swaps in only if neither field has been touched. If the user typed anything, the model's answer quietly becomes alternative slot one.

07. How the pages connect

Six sections of this deck each show one screen. This one shows the wiring: which surfaces exist, every path between them, and the single loop the whole product is built to make fast. No new pixels here — only the graph they sit in.

Insidor has three tabs and three pushed routes. That is the entire surface area. Live feed, Search and You are tabs, always reachable, always in the same place. Story, Coin and Create are pushed routes with their own URLs — they are not tabs, because the lead-time claim is unfalsifiable unless a stranger can open the link and check it, and because a modal cannot be sent to a colleague. Everything else people expect to be a page — the coin lanes, the bands, the filters, the ⌘K palette, the evidence popover, the swap confirm — is either URL state on /feed or an overlay that closes back to the page underneath.

The navigation graph. Notice that Story, Coin and Create are each reachable from at least three places and each lead somewhere forward as well as back — the graph has no leaf. The back chip is drawn dashed because it carries the real referrer (← Live feed, ← Search, ← You) rather than defaulting to the feed, which is what stops a user landing on a shared story link and being dumped somewhere they have never been.

Two rules do most of the work here. First, stories never buy; coins buy. The Story page's confirmed-coin row navigates to /coin/<mint> and the swap happens there. Keeping one surface for money means the safety gates, the quote lifecycle and the fee disclosure exist once rather than in three places drifting apart. Second, Create is entered, never browsed. There is no "Create" tab; the route only opens from a place where the coin count is genuinely zero, and the server re-checks that count at submit — so nobody pays rent to mint a duplicate of a coin the page was a few seconds behind on.

The core loop. The two cyan segments are the only places conviction actually climbs; everything before is triage and everything after is bookkeeping. If the climb from SCAN to ACT takes longer than about ninety seconds, the head start the product sells has already been spent on reading.

The loop is drawn as a line, not a ring, because it is not symmetrical: most laps end at 02. A user scans forty rows and judges none of them, and that has to be a cheap, non-guilty exit — which is why every row is 54px of readable facts rather than a card that demands a decision. The rows that survive triage go to a page whose entire job is to answer two questions and then present exactly one control.

The fork. One pure function, no I/O, unit-tested: the count of confirmed matches selects the button, and nothing else may. The amber panel on the right is the state that matters most — the two Atletico Kang candidates are drawn at full brightness, labelled unverified, and given exactly one action. Greying them out would read as broken; hiding them would be dishonest; putting a Buy on them would sell a claim we declined to make.

Three guards in that filter are load-bearing rather than defensive. mint_time null means we cannot state the coin's age, and age is a headline number on a board that sells earliness. ticker is denormalised onto the match row because the table it originally comes from is truncated and rewritten every enrichment cycle — a page load racing that cycle would otherwise render Buy $undefined. And confirmation is an identity assertion, not a safety one, which is why the n = 1 branch has a second gate: a rugged coin stays confirmed forever, and without the tradeability check it would render a Buy button forever too.

The journey. The cyan wall is where Insidor's board lit up; the amber wall is where DexScreener, Axiom and Photon become useful at all. The 34 minutes between them is the entire product, and it is the number the whole system exists to measure honestly — including when it comes out negative.

In plain language: at 09:00 a Norwegian ferry captain retires and somebody films the horn. It is a normal post on a normal account, and no part of crypto is watching. At 09:04 our ingest catches it and writes one snapshot — 41,000 views. That number is useless on its own; a single point has no velocity and we deliberately do nothing with it. At 09:09 the second snapshot lands at 118,000 and the trigger fires on the rate, not the size. Nine minutes is our floor and it is a floor for a reason: measure faster and you are reacting to noise, measure slower and you have given the head start away.

At 09:12 a second account, on a different platform, posts its own clip of the same horn. That is the birth quorum — two authors, two platforms — and it is the check that separates a story from one account having a good day. At 09:13, sixty seconds later, promoted_at is written once and never moved again, /story/8814 starts existing at a URL, and the row appears in the stories lane with no coin against it. That single write is the origin of every earliness claim the product will ever make about this story.

At 09:31 somebody reading our board mints $FERRY — eighteen minutes after the story appeared, and while the only people who know it is a story are the ones looking at Insidor. At 09:47 crypto Twitter finally arrives, t_crypto fires, and the measurement closes at +34 minutes. From that moment the coin is on every screener and the edge is gone. Sixteen of those minutes were still spendable after the coin existed — that gap, between our wall and theirs, is the only thing being sold here, and the reason every screen in this deck is rendered at 10:02, thirty-one minutes into the coin's life and still inside the window.

08. The algorithm — how we find viral posts

There is no feed of everything. The detector is a budget-allocation problem wearing a statistics costume: we can afford to look at roughly one post in a thousand, and the product is the discipline of choosing which thousandth.

1. The constraint

X publishes on the order of half a billion posts a day. Nobody reads them all and no vendor sells them all. Our reseller bills about $0.00015 per post returned, so a defensible $75/day sensor budget buys roughly 500,000 reads — one per thousand posts published. Worse than it sounds, because a read is not a measurement: to know something is growing you must read it twice, which puts the ceiling on distinct measurable posts nearer one in five thousand.

So the first design decision is not a model, it is a filter that costs nothing. Six arithmetic rules over the arriving payload — roster, media, length, named entity, minus bait regex, minus an image seen in the last 30 days — drop ~70% before a single paid call.

THE FUNNEL — one day, X only · widths illustrative, ratios exact

The top strip is a day of X drawn to scale; the cyan hairline is the whole budget. The expensive part is not the top of the funnel but the middle, where 30,000 posts each need a second look before any of them can be ranked.

2. Why the obvious approach fails

The obvious detector is a saved search — min_faves:100, polled, alert on what returns. One line of code, and what most competitors run. It cannot work for us for an arithmetic reason: a post does not cross 100 likes until it has been travelling twenty to sixty minutes, by which point the coin is frequently already minted. A threshold search does not find viral posts early. It finds posts that have already gone viral and sells you the receipt.

TIMELINE — median case · minutes since the post was published

Same post, two detectors. Ours decides at minute 9 and shows at minute 12. A 100-like search cannot resolve the post before minute 20 and often not before minute 60 — the wrong side of the mint. The product lives in that gap.

3. What we do instead — three lanes

Rather than raising the engagement bar to cut volume, we drop it to the floor and cut volume with the free arrival prior instead. A min_faves:15 net over a rolling six-minute window, re-run every three minutes, is account-agnostic. That matters because no roster covers the case we most need: a brand-new account, or a dev wallet's first post. The net removes ~99% of raw volume while keeping essentially everything that could matter. Beside it sit a curated push stream on ~500 known amplifiers — the only path that delivers in seconds — and TikTok topic surfaces, because TikTok has no firehose and must be polled by subject.

THREE LANES — different jobs, one admit gate

Lane B is the one people underrate: cheapest of the three, and the only one that can see an account we have never heard of — precisely the account that posts the clip a coin gets minted from.

4. The measurement — a geometric snapshot grid

Once admitted, a post is re-read on a fixed, widening schedule: 4, 9, 14, 21, 30, 42, 58 and 78 minutes, each gap about 1.5× the last. Early on everything moves and observations are worth a lot; an hour in the trajectory is set and dense sampling is money burnt.

This is also where the earliest-possible alert comes from, and it is worth being blunt: one observation is a level, not a rate. A post at 900 views at minute 4 might be decelerating from 800 views/min or accelerating from 40 — you cannot tell, at any price, with any model. The first honest rate exists at τ=9, and no infrastructure spend moves that floor. It is physics, not engineering. Everything the roadmap can improve sits after minute 9.

SNAPSHOT GRID — τ, minutes since publish · ratio ≈ 1.5

Nothing before the cyan tick is a signal, however big the number looks. Designing the alert around this prevents the classic failure: firing on one large reading that turns out to be the tail of something which peaked before we arrived.

5. The signal — rate relative to the author, then acceleration

Raw numbers are nearly meaningless. 10,000 views in ten minutes is a thunderclap for a 512-follower ferry-spotting account and a quiet Tuesday for one with 2M followers. So every rate is scored against that author's own recent baseline. A thin-history account gets a wide band borrowed from its follower bucket, which stops us mistaking ignorance for signal. Then we take the second derivative: two posts can sit at identical levels while one is compounding and the other has turned over, and acceleration is the only thing that separates heating from already peaked.

TWO WAYS TO READ THE SAME NUMBER

Left: why a small account can outrank a huge one. Right: why the board ranks on acceleration rather than size — the amber curve is the "found it after it was over" failure, and level alone cannot detect it.

6. Is any of this machine learning?

Mostly no, and we should say so plainly rather than let anyone assume a trained model exists in here that does not. Detection and ranking are classical statistics: rates, baselines, quantiles. Judgement calls that need language understanding go to Claude. Similarity uses an off-the-shelf embedding model exactly as sold. Exactly one model is fit by us — a logistic regression that turns a matching score into a calibrated probability. No training runs, no GPUs, no labelled corpus beyond a few hundred hand-checked pairs.

7. Proving we were early

A lead-time number is trivially faked by measuring it backwards. Alert first, then go looking for the coin, and we choose which coin counts and when the clock started — lookahead bias, and it turns the headline number into a lie. So both reference clocks run ahead of the pipeline, independently of it: a websocket records every Solana mint as it happens, a poll over ~300 crypto-Twitter accounts records every ticker mention, and both indexes are written before any alert fires. promoted_at is stamped once and never overwritten; lead time is the distance to the first entry either clock already holds.

TWO PROSPECTIVE CLOCKS — both written before the alert

Both tapes are recording continuously whether or not we ever alert. That is what makes the lead-time figure an observation rather than a claim — and it is why we can publish the misses alongside it.

One consequence the team should internalise. A post first seen when it is already large is permanently marked ineligible for the earliness statistic. It can still appear on the board under an "Already Hot" label, but it never counts toward lead time. Any other choice quietly inflates the one number the product is sold on.

09. How the database is set up

One Postgres, one public schema, 46 tables. Its single job is to make a wrong state unrepresentable — not merely discouraged by careful coding — because every defect the last build shipped passed code review.

The schema divides cleanly into four families. The pipeline's tables hold what we observed: a story, the posts clustered into it, the counter readings taken from those posts over time. The market's tables hold what Solana actually did: every mint the stream saw, its price and liquidity series, its safety signals, and the matcher's verdict linking a mint to a story. The clock and record tables hold the product's one falsifiable claim — how early we were, and whether it worked out. The user's tables hold what a person did: followed, held, commented, traded, launched.

Naming was decided once so nobody has to remember a list: tables are singular, enums are singular, columns are snake_case, and every foreign key to a story is story_id. The route is /story/[id] and the copy on every screen says story, so the schema says story too — narrative is vocabulary from a codebase being deleted, and a lint rule bans the identifier in both apps.

Notice what is not connected. story_ticker holds candidate tickers; it has no market columns at all and only a dashed, nullable link to coin. coin_match is the only table the Buy button reads. In the old schema those were one row in one table — which is exactly how an LLM-invented ticker got resolved against a live market. Notice also that story_clock has four inbound edges and no outbound write path: every input to a lead-time claim is a foreign key or a NOT NULL watermark, and the claim itself is a generated column.

The constraints that matter

This is the part worth understanding, because it is where the product's rules actually live. Each of the four below corresponds to a specific defect the previous build shipped. In every case the fix is not a better code review — it is a column definition or a CHECK that makes the bad row something Postgres refuses to store.

Rule 01 · ticker provenance0004_matching.sql

A ticker with no provenance cannot be inserted at all.

source ticker_source NOT NULL   -- no DEFAULT
CHECK (source <> 'llm' OR resolved_mint IS NULL)
CHECK (source <> 'llm' OR source_span IS NOT NULL)
PreventsAn INSERT that forgets to say where a ticker came from fails. There is no value the column can hold meaning “we don't know.” And a ticker the model invented can never point at a real mint — which is the exact defect that produced a Buy button on an unrelated coin.
Rule 02 · lead-time immutability0002 · 0005

promoted_at is set once and never moves.

CREATE TRIGGER story_freeze_promoted_at
  BEFORE UPDATE ON public.story
  FOR EACH ROW EXECUTE FUNCTION
    insidor.freeze_columns('promoted_at');
PreventsLead time is the product's whole claim. A column anyone can quietly overwrite is a claim anyone can quietly edit. The same trigger freezes coin.minted_at, story_clock.t_mint and t_ct, so a later sighting can never move an earlier one.
Rule 03 · evidence before confidence0004_matching.sql

A match cannot be CONFIRMED without the evidence that justifies it.

CHECK (verdict <> 'confirmed' OR (
  score >= 0.80
  AND ticker_source <> 'llm'
  AND mint_time IS NOT NULL
  AND relation <> 'mentioned'
  AND (s_mint_in_post >= 0.90 OR strong >= 2)))
PreventsA confident-looking verdict backed by one weak signal. Below the bar the row is still stored — as unsure, which is what renders the abstain state with the Buy button suppressed. Abstaining is a first-class outcome, not a missing row.
Rule 04 · absent is not zero0003 · 0005 · 0010

Missing data cannot be stored — or rendered — as zero.

CHECK ((minted_at IS NULL) = (minted_at_source = 'unknown'))
CHECK (price_usd IS NULL OR price_usd > 0)  -- $0 ≠ absent
CHECK ((value IS NULL) = (state = 'unknown'))
CHECK (burst IS NULL OR ticks_qualified >= 2)
PreventsA coin of unknown age reading “0m old” and looking brand new. Every counter is nullable, an unknown authority is the literal 'unknown' and fails the gate closed, and ?? 0 is a lint error all the way out to the React component, which renders an em dash and a reason.

Four constraints, four shipped defects. Note the shape they share: none of them asks a developer to remember anything. Rule 03 is written out in full rather than calling a function, because a CHECK depending on a user-defined function is a dump/restore ordering hazard, and this is the one constraint that must never fail to restore.

Migrations

The old build had 24 loose schema-*.sql files applied by 12 ad-hoc scripts, with no ordering and no record of what had actually been run against which database. Nobody could answer “what is live?” without reading the tables. That is now fourteen ordered files and one ledger.

Every file from 0001 on opens with insidor.migration_begin() and closes with migration_end(), inside one transaction. migration_begin takes a transaction-scoped advisory lock, so two deploys racing serialise instead of interleaving DDL; refuses to apply 0007 if 0008 is already recorded; and refuses to re-apply a file whose bytes changed after it was applied. Every statement inside is idempotent, so migrate.mjs verify can re-run the entire set and assert it is a no-op — if it isn't, the DDL is wrong and CI fails there rather than during a production deploy.

Three things follow from that “not applied” flag, and the team should hear them plainly. First, every generated column here is a bet: Postgres checks generation expressions for immutability at DDL time, so a single STABLE function in one of them fails the migration on first apply, loudly and immediately — 0009's typed wrapper around array_to_string exists precisely because of that. Second, CHECK constraints get no such check, so the immutable-safe style used throughout is backed by a dump→restore round trip in CI rather than by discipline. Third, the authoring rules in ARCHITECTURE.md §4.4 require a @phase, @lock, @ticket and @summary header on every file, and the files as committed do not yet carry them — so check-migrations.mjs will fail on the first run and fourteen headers need writing before the first apply. That is the correct order of events: the gate fails before the database does.

10. How each feature gets built

Every screen in the previous nine sections has to come out of a folder that someone can find at 3am. This section is the map of those folders, the order they get built in, and the three questions only the founder can answer before the order holds.

The repository is one npm workspace containing two things that deploy separately and never import each other. apps/web is the site: Next.js, the only thing Vercel builds. apps/pipeline is the machine that never sleeps: ingestion, scoring, clustering, the coin matcher, the two earliness clocks, the board commit that reranks every twenty seconds. They share a database and a package of type definitions, and nothing else. That separation is not tidiness — it is what stops a slow scraper from taking the website down with it, and what stops a website deploy from silently restarting the clocks that our central claim is measured against.

Inside apps/web the code is divided by feature, not by kind of file. There is no components/ drawer where four hundred files go to be forgotten. There are eleven feature folders, each of which owns one part of the product completely, and each of which exposes exactly two public doors: index.ts for anything safe to send to a browser, and server.ts for anything that touches the database. Everything else in the folder is private. A file in feed/ cannot reach inside trading/ and grab a helper; it can only knock on one of the two doors. This is checked by a lint rule reading a single JSON file, so adding a new connection between two features is a one-line diff a reviewer can argue with, rather than an import someone slipped in on a Friday.

Why pure logic is pulled out

A rule sitting inside a React component can only be checked by building a browser, loading a page and looking at it. The same rule as a function is checked by calling it. Every judgement that costs a user money — is this match trustworthy, what is the fee, is this row eligible, does Buy or Create appear — is a function, so it can be proven rather than eyeballed.

Why the segments are flat

Folders inside folders are where files hide. Flat means the answer to "which file is this bug in" is visible in one screen of the tree. ui/ gets exactly one level, because the two lanes genuinely are two component families.

Why ui/ never fetches

Components receive finished view-models, never an id to go look up. A component that can fetch is a component that can fetch on a board of forty rows.

The build sequence

The seven surfaces do not get built in the order a user meets them. They get built in the order the data allows, and the order is forced by three facts. The mint address has to exist before anything can be bought, matched or searched — today the code resolves one and throws it away, which is why the very first migration is a single column. The coinability tier has to be populated and evaluated before any public board ships, because the "no coin yet" list is a browsable, one-tap-mintable index and it is the most exposed surface in the product. And both earliness clocks have to be running, with gap detection, before a single lead-time number renders anywhere — a lead time computed from one live sensor is not a measurement, it is a guess with a decimal point.

Search sits last for a reason worth naming out loud, because it is the one people push back on: search cannot ship before the coin index. Until there is an index of every mint on the chain — including the thousands that never touched a story — searching a ticker returns the coins we happen to know about, and confidently answers "no coin exists for this" when three do. That is not a thin feature, it is a wrong answer on the exact question the box is there to settle, and it is the answer someone acts on by minting a duplicate.

Does the stories lane stay inside Live feed?

step 3, the posts lane.

02

Does the poster's 40% ship?

Forty percent of a coin's fee stream to the person whose post it came from, at an address derived from their handle, whether or not they have ever heard of Insidor. It is the most defensible thing about the product and the largest legal surface. Engineering is already settled: a sharing config is created on every launch, at roughly 0.004 SOL each, purely to keep the option alive — but the shares themselves cannot be written until you decide.

cost of deciding late

irreversible, per coin — a mint launched without a share configuration can never have one added. Every coin created before the decision is permanently split-less.

the asymmetry

Deciding no later costs nothing. Deciding yes later is impossible for everything already minted.

03

What is the coinability rule for someone who was private until Tuesday?

The tier system permits public figures and stage names and forbids identifiable private individuals. The modal Insidor story is a person who had no public presence at all four days ago and now has two million views — a retiring ferry captain, a barista, someone's grandmother. This needs a written rule from you, not a model judgement, because it is the tier that will actually bite and the one where being wrong is unrecoverable.

cost of deciding late

blocks every public board — nothing ships on a public URL before coinability is populated and evaluated. The rule lives in the scoring prompt, not a keyword blocklist, so it can be changed the day you write it — but it cannot be absent.

needed by

step 3. It is the earliest hard blocker on the list.
