01. What Insidor is

Memecoins are made out of things going viral. Every tool a trader uses today shows them a coin only after it exists and money is already moving into it. Insidor works one step earlier: we watch the internet, not the money, and show people the story before anyone has made a coin out of it.

The problem

Axiom, Photon and GMGN are all screens full of coins. They are good screens. But a coin can only appear on them once somebody has already created it, and by then the interesting part has happened. The first buyers are in. The chart already has a shape. What you are looking at is not an opportunity, it is the aftermath of one.

That has a blunt consequence. If you are consistently arriving after the coin exists, you are not early — you are the person the early buyers sell to. Being fast on those tools only makes you the quickest of the late.

The idea

Every one of those coins started as something on the internet: a clip, a photo, a sentence that made a lot of people laugh at the same time. That thing is public, it is free to look at, and it exists before the coin does. So instead of watching the money, we watch the thing the money will eventually chase. We scan social media, spot posts that are exploding right now, and put them in front of traders while there is still nothing to buy.

Where each tool picks up the story Harbor Last Horn · real sequence

One story, one clock. Everyone else's product begins at 09:47, where the coin already exists. Ours begins at 09:13, where there is only a video of a ferry. The 34 minutes in between is the entire company.

Two ways to win

Arriving that early leaves the user with two moves, and Insidor supports both in the same place. That matters: nobody wants to spot a story here, then go somewhere else to act on it.

Why it is defensible

Speed alone is not a moat — anyone can build something fast. What protects Insidor is that two assets get bigger every day the product runs, and a competitor starting tomorrow starts with none of either.

COMPOUND #1 · THE COMMUNITY
Their thesis history lives here

Traders post their reasoning on each story — why this one is real, why that one is nothing. Over months that becomes a public track record with their name on it.

People do not abandon a reputation they spent a year building. The writing is the lock-in, and we are not the ones who have to produce it.

COMPOUND #2 · THE RECORD
Which viral posts became coins that made money

Every story we surface, we keep: what it looked like as it climbed, whether a coin followed, and how that coin did. Nobody sells this. You cannot go back and ask what a post looked like twenty minutes ago — if it was not recorded live, it is gone.

A competitor can copy the screen in a fortnight. They cannot copy a year of yesterdays.

Who this is for

THE TRADER
Wants to be first, not fast

Already good at reading a chart, already has Axiom open. What they do not have is anything that fires before the chart exists. That is the only thing we sell them.

THE CREATOR
Wants to mint off a meme

Their whole job is finding the funny thing early. Today that means scrolling all day and hoping. Here the candidates arrive already sorted, with a name and ticker suggested.

THE NEWCOMER
Saw it on TikTok, wants in

No wallet, no jargon, no idea what a bonding curve is. They get a page that explains the story in one sentence and a button that works. Everything else stays out of the way.

Three people, one screen. The trader reads it for the timing, the creator for the raw material, the newcomer for the sentence at the top. Nothing on the page is written for an insider only.

02. The narratives feed

The home screen. A single list of the stories going viral on the internet right now, best one at the top, each one explained in two lines of plain English so you never have to watch the video to know what it is.

Every other tool a trader opens — Axiom, Photon, GMGN — is a list of coins. Insidor's home screen is a list of stories, and most of them do not have a coin yet. That is the whole point. If a row already has money flowing into it, you were late. The feed's job is to put the ferry captain in front of you at 09:13, eighteen minutes before anybody turns it into a coin called $FERRY and thirty-four minutes before crypto Twitter notices.

The order of the list is the product. Behind it sits everything described in section 01 — how fast views are climbing, how many separate people are posting the same thing, whether the AI thinks a coin could plausibly be made out of it. None of that appears on screen. The user gets the order; the order is the answer. Putting our numbers next to the rows would hand the judgement back to the person we are meant to be making it for.

The two lines are the product

The most important text in the whole app is the sentence under each title. "A retiring ferry captain blew his horn one last time, and every boat in the harbour blew back." Read that and you already know whether it is worth a coin — you can feel whether it is funny, whether it has a name in it, whether people will repeat it. So it is set larger and brighter than the title above it. It is not a subtitle. It is the row.

Everything else on the row is a fact about the world that would be true whether or not Insidor existed: how many people have seen it, whether the line is still going up, how old it is, and what you can do about it. Seven things, no eighth.

The seven elements, and why each one earns its place

1
A picture from the post

The still frame from the video or image. It is how you recognise a story you have already seen scrolling elsewhere, in a fifth of a second, before reading anything.

2
A title

"Harbor Last Horn." A short handle so the story can be referred to, searched for, and pasted into a group chat. Deliberately set smaller than the sentence below it.

3
Two lines of plain English

The point of the entire screen. You should understand the story without watching the video. Largest, brightest text in the row — it is what you actually judge the trade on.

4
How many views

The headline number, big and unambiguous. A true fact about the world: this many people have seen it. Not our estimate of anything, just the count.

5
A tiny line graph

The view count over the last hour. Answers the one question a single number cannot: is it still going up, or is it over? Cyan and rising, or grey and flat.

6
How old it is

Four hours. Six hours. Combined with the graph this is the whole timing story — 850K in two hours is a different animal from 850K in two days.

7
One button

Create coin if nobody has made one, Buy $TICKER if somebody has, 3 coins → if several exist and you need to pick. One row, one obvious next move.

Every number on it was real. It still had to go, because it asks the user to do the job we built the company to do for them.

03. The narrative page

The screen the whole product is built around. One story, one page: what happened, how big it is now, which coins were made from it, where to buy, and what other traders think.

Every narrative gets its own web address — insidor.app/n/harbor-last-horn. A trader pastes that link into a group chat, and someone who has never heard of us opens it, understands the story in one sentence, and can act. Nothing on it needs an account to read. The page is the marketing.

It answers four questions in one place: what is this · is it still hot · which coin is the real one · what do other traders think. Today those answers live in four separate tabs — a video app, a chart, a token scanner, a group chat. Putting them on one page is most of the product.

The story, and how viral it is

The top of the page is the clip and the plain-English two-liner. That sentence is the most important text in the product — it is what a stranger reads first. Beside it sit facts only: views, reposts, likes, age, and the view counts we recorded ourselves as the story grew. Nobody sells "what did this post look like twenty minutes ago", so we write it down as it happens and show it back plainly.

Why every coin is listed, not just one

When something goes viral, several people make a coin within minutes. One is the real one the crowd settles on; the rest are copycats riding the attention. Showing only "the coin" would mean a user sometimes buys a copycat and loses money because of a silent decision of ours. So we list all of them, labelled: First — made from this story before any other. Biggest — the most money in it now. Those are usually different coins, and which one you want depends on how you trade.

The third label matters most. Unverified means we think a coin relates to this story but we are not confident, and it gets no buy button at all — not a greyed-out one. In the founder's words: "A greyed-out button looks like something we're holding back; no button at all is us not making a claim we can't stand behind."

Trading and research, on the same page

Buying happens here, not in another app — and we rent the exchange rather than build it. The plan is to route orders through Axiom or Photon, pending confirmation they permit it; neither publishes a public developer interface today, so the confirmed fallback is Jupiter, which works now and pays us a fee for the business. Wallets are handled by Privy; charts, safety checks and holder data by DexScreener, RugCheck and Helius. All plumbing. What we build is the part above — finding the story before the coin existed.

The thesis layer

At the bottom, traders write what they think. Two facts attach automatically at the moment of posting and can never be edited: how big the story was when they said it, and whether they own the coin — holding, sold, or no position, read from the connected wallet. Someone who called this at 340,000 views reads very differently from someone who turned up at 2.4 million; someone talking up a coin they hold reads differently from someone with nothing on the table. Neither is disqualifying. Both are things you would want to know.

Why nobody else can build this layer

It looks like a comment section. It is not, because of who can attach those two facts. Twitter knows what people say but not what they own. A trading app knows what they own but not what they think. Insidor is the only place the take and the wallet arrive together, on the same story, at the same moment — so the record writes itself and compounds. That history is why traders stay: their calls and their reputation live here.

The same argument as the view-count history: the only way to have it is to have been recording since day one.

What is deliberately not on this page

None of our own scoring. No heat number, no acceleration figure, no confidence percentage on the unverified coin. The page carries facts about the world and our decisions — which story to show, which coins to list, which one gets a button. The user gets the judgement, not the arithmetic.

04. Create a coin

Every other tool shows a coin after it exists. We show the story before it exists — so we can offer something nobody else can: the user makes the coin themselves, from the story, in a few taps, and is first in.

This is the single biggest thing that separates Insidor from Axiom, Photon or GMGN. Those tools are fed by money moving. A coin has to already be trading before it can appear on them, which means every user of those tools arrives after somebody else got there. We are fed by the internet instead, so we see the ferry captain, the plastic plant, the town paying people to move — while the coin is still an idea. At that moment the user has two doors. If a coin exists, they buy it (section 03). If none does, they open this sheet.

The example here is "Paid to Move to Norway" — a Norwegian town is paying people €25,000 to move there. 1.1M views, an hour old, and nobody has made a coin out of it yet.

Where the pre-filled name comes from

The user does not sit and think of a name. The work was already done, about an hour earlier, by a part of the system they never see. When our AI reader looked at this post it was answering one question — could someone realistically make a memecoin out of this? — and part of answering it is proposing what the coin would be called. That suggestion was written down at the time and stored with the story. By the time somebody taps Create, the name, the ticker and the image have been sitting there ready for an hour.

The whole left half of this line happens without anybody asking for it. Naming is a by-product of judging, so it costs nothing extra and it is finished long before a human arrives.

05. Trading, watchlist, search and trending

Four supporting features. None of them is the reason someone opens Insidor, but without them the person who spots a story early has to leave to act on it, forgets to come back, accidentally makes a coin that already exists, or can only see what we choose to push at them.

Trading — buying built into the page

When a narrative already has a coin, the buy box sits on the same page as the story. No copying a contract address into another app, no second tab, no losing the thing you were looking at. You read the two-liner, you look at the numbers, you buy. We take a small fee on every trade that goes through us, and that fee is shown in the order before you confirm — nothing hidden in the price.

We do not build the trading engine. Actually executing a trade on-chain — routing it, getting a good price, handling failures, not losing people's money — is hard, risky, and already solved by companies who do only that. The plan is to route orders through Axiom or Photon, pending confirmation that they permit it: research found neither publishes a public developer API, so the integration is not yet proven. The confirmed fallback is Jupiter, which is openly documented and pays us an integrator fee, where Photon charges the user 1% and pays us nothing.

06. The six parts of the machine

Everything behind the app is six moving parts. Four of them run on their own, on a schedule, with nobody watching. One is a database in the middle. The last one is the app itself. This section is the part of Insidor the user never sees.

The job the machine has to do is narrow: find a post that is going viral right now, work out whether anyone could make a memecoin out of it, and notice when lots of separate posts are all about the same thing. Everything else — the prices, the charts, the wallet, the buying — is rented from companies who already do it well. So the machine is small on purpose. Six parts, and only two of them are things nobody else can do for us.

They pass work along a line. The Collector brings in candidate posts. The Measurer watches those posts over time. The Judge reads the ones that are moving fastest. The Grouper bundles related ones together into a single story. All four write what they find into the Library, which is one database and the single source of truth. The App only ever reads out of the Library. It never talks to X, or to Claude, or to the data reseller directly.

How work flows — four writers, one Library, one reader

Work moves left to right along the top, and everything drains down into one database. The App sits underneath and only reads. If a job dies overnight, the Library still has everything it wrote before it died, and the App carries on serving it.

1 · The Collector

Runs every couple of minutes. It asks a data reseller — a company that already has a firehose of social posts and sells access to it — for posts that are getting a lot of attention right now. It cannot look at everything: hundreds of millions of posts are made every day, and paying to read all of them would cost more than the company is worth. So it filters hard before anything else happens, and only a small shortlist survives to the next step.

2 · The Measurer — the part that is easy to skip and impossible to replace

The Measurer takes every post the Collector shortlisted and looks at it again a few minutes later. And again. And again, for the first several hours of its life. Each time, it writes down one thing: how many views the post has, and the exact minute it checked.

This sounds trivial and it is the whole product. Here is why. A single view count tells you almost nothing. Five hundred thousand views could mean the post exploded in ten minutes — in which case it is happening right now and you want in — or that it crawled to the same number over two days, in which case it is finished and everyone who cared already saw it. The number on the post is identical in both cases. Only by measuring the same post twice can you tell the difference.

Two posts, the same 500,000 views, opposite meanings

Both posts hit exactly 500,000. A tool that reads the number once cannot tell them apart. The difference only exists in the gap between two readings — and that gap is what the Measurer is manufacturing, five minutes at a time.

Why this is urgent

This history cannot be bought later. Nobody sells "what did this post look like twenty minutes ago."

You can buy today's view count from a dozen places. You cannot buy the shape of how it got there, because nobody was writing it down. If we are not recording it as it happens, it is gone forever — and every day we run, the record gets longer and a competitor starting tomorrow starts with none of it.

3 · The Judge

Claude reads the posts that are climbing fastest and answers one question: could someone realistically make a memecoin out of this? Most viral posts are useless to us. Politics, sport, a plane crash, a news bulletin — huge numbers, nothing to name a coin after. What works is one absurd thing that has a name: a frog called Gerald, a plastic plant, a ferry horn. When the answer is yes, the Judge also proposes a coin name and a ticker. Understanding humour is exactly the sort of thing a language model is for, and exactly the sort of thing a rule you write by hand will never do.

4 · The Grouper

One person posting a video of a Norwegian ferry captain is luck. Twenty people independently posting about the same captain, on two different platforms, within an hour, is a meme actually forming. The Grouper spots that ten separate posts are about the same thing and bundles them into one narrative — which is the unit the whole app is built around. Without it the feed is ten near-identical rows about a ferry. With it, there is one story with ten pieces of evidence behind it.

5 · The Library, and 6 · The App

The Library is one database. Everything the four jobs learn goes in there and nowhere else, which means there is never a question of which copy is right. The App reads narratives out of it and puts them on screen. That is the only direction data moves: the App never reaches out to a social network or an AI itself, so a slow reseller or a failed AI call can never make the page hang.

Where all of this lives

The first four are not something anyone runs. They are scheduled jobs on a server: they fire on a timetable, whether or not our laptops are open, whether or not anyone is using the app. Nobody presses anything. If one fails it retries on its next turn, and the Library keeps whatever it already wrote.

07. What we build, what we rent

One sentence carries this whole section: we build the detection, we rent everything else. Almost every moving part of Insidor already exists as a service somebody else runs better than we would. We pay for those and spend all of our own effort on the one thing nobody sells.

A small team's only real asset is where it points its attention. Every week spent writing a wallet system, a price chart or a trading engine is a week not spent on the reason the product exists. All of those things are solved problems with mature providers behind them; the thing we are trying to do — see a story going viral before a coin is made out of it — is not sold by anyone, because nobody else is recording it.

So the rule for every job in the system is the same question: can we buy this? If yes, we buy it and move on. If no, that is where the company lives.

08. What we store, and what it costs

Six kinds of thing get written down, and the whole machine runs on about five pounds a day. This section is the honest accounting: what sits in the database, why each piece has to be there, and where every pound goes.

The database — the Library, part five of the machine — is one central store and the single source of truth. Everything else reads from it. There are six kinds of record. Narratives are the stories themselves: one entry per viral moment, holding the plain-English two-liner and the pointers to everything else. Posts are the individual social posts that make up a story — the original X post, the TikTok clip someone else put up twenty minutes later. View readings are the important one: every single time the Measurer re-checked a post, we write down the clock time and the view count. That is what draws the climbing line, and it is the one thing we could never buy back later. Nobody sells "what did this post look like twenty minutes ago". If we are not recording it as it happens, it is gone.

Coins are every coin we have tied to a story, with a note of how sure we are about the link — some are obvious, some are a guess, and we keep the difference. Comments are the traders' theses, each one stamped with how big the story was at the moment it was written, frozen permanently so a call made early can never be quietly reframed as a call made late. Running costs are the day's spending on data and AI, written down as we go, so the system knows what it has spent and can stop itself.

09. Where the line sits

Part three is where the two halves of this document meet. Everything before this was the machine; everything after is the screen. Exactly one rule governs what is allowed to cross between them.

The rule is short. Facts about the world go on screen. Our reasoning never does. A view count is a fact — the post really has 2.4 million views whether or not Insidor was ever built. A market cap is a fact. So is the time a post went up, the name of the account that posted it, and how many people hold a coin. All of those existed before we looked and would carry on existing if we shut down tomorrow.

Our reasoning is a different kind of thing. When our system decides a story is worth showing, it is doing arithmetic: comparing one view count against the same post's view count twenty minutes earlier, weighing how many separate people posted about the same ferry captain, asking the AI how memeable the thing is and getting a number back. Those numbers are real and they matter enormously — they are how the ordering is decided — but they exist only because we invented them. They are our workings, and workings do not belong in front of a user.

In the founder's words: "The app's job is to make the judgement so the user doesn't have to. Showing our workings would just make them do our job for us." That is the whole argument. A trader opening Insidor at 09:13 has perhaps ninety seconds before the window closes. If we hand them a heat score of 8.4 and an acceleration ratio of 3.1x, we have not helped them decide — we have handed them a second decision on top of the first one, and asked them to learn our internal scale before they can use the product. The correct output of all that arithmetic is not a number. It is an ordered list and one plain sentence.

10. One story, end to end

One morning, minute by minute. This is the Harbor Last Horn story from Part One with the six-part machine from Part Two running underneath it, so you can see exactly which part was doing what, and when.

At 09:00 a Norwegian account called @nordkyst_ferge posts a clip. A retiring ferry captain blows his horn one last time, and every other boat in the harbour blows theirs back. It is a lovely forty seconds of video. Nobody in crypto is looking at it, because as far as crypto is concerned there is nothing there to look at — no coin, no chart, no money moving. Every tool a memecoin trader uses starts working at the moment money starts moving. On this particular morning, that moment is 09:47.

Insidor is looking at 09:04.

Seven moments, drawn to real time. Four of them happen inside nine minutes, which is why the leader lines fan the way they do — the cards are evenly spaced, the moments are not. The cyan band is the product. Everything below the line is the machinery from Part Two, running.

What the user actually did

Almost nothing, which is the point. At around 09:13 they opened Insidor while waiting for a coffee. Near the top of the feed was a card with one sentence on it — “A retiring ferry captain blew his horn one last time, and every boat in the harbour blew back” — a still from the clip, the title Harbor Last Horn, the view count, a tiny line graph still bending upwards, how old it was, and one button reading Create coin, because nobody had made one yet. Seven things, exactly as in Section 2. Who posted it comes on the next screen, when they tap through.

They read the sentence, watched the clip, and decided the horn was funny enough that other people would find it funny too. That took about eighteen minutes of thinking about it and going back to it. At 09:31 they tapped Create. The tap opened pump.fun with a name and ticker already filled in and their wallet already connected through Privy, and $FERRY existed. They were the first person in the world to make a coin out of that video. Sixteen minutes later the rest of crypto Twitter arrived and found a coin that was already there.

What was happening while they did it

At 09:04 the Collector did what it does every couple of minutes: asked our data reseller for posts pulling unusual attention right now. The ferry clip came back with 41,000 views. On its own that number means nothing — it could be a post that exploded in ten minutes or one that crawled there over two days.

So the Measurer put it on a list and looked at it again. At 09:09 the same post read 118,000 views. That second reading is the whole trick: two numbers on the same post five minutes apart is the difference between a story that is still climbing and one that is already over. Nobody will sell us that comparison later. If we are not writing it down as it happens, it is gone.

The Judge then read the post and answered one question — could a person realistically make a memecoin out of this? Most fast-growing posts fail that test: politics, sport, a plane crash. A retiring captain and a harbour full of horns passes it easily, and it comes with a name attached. At 09:12 a second clip of the same moment turned up on TikTok from a different account, and the Grouper tied the two together into one narrative. One person posting is luck. Two independent people posting the same moment is a meme forming.

Every reading, every judgement and both source posts went into the Library as they happened, which is why at 09:13 the App could put the story in front of every user with nothing missing. And it is why, months from now, this morning will be one row in the record that powers Stage 7 — the last stage of the build plan in Section 11, the one that can finally answer “what happened to stories like this one”. It only works if we started writing the rows down today.

Why the 34 minutes is the company

Axiom, Photon and GMGN are all excellent, and on this morning every one of them was correct and useless. They watch money. There was no money to watch until 09:47. We watch the internet, so we were reading the story at 09:04 and showing it at 09:13. The gap between those two facts is the entire product — and on the days when a coin already exists by the time we surface the story, the same gap simply becomes a better entry price instead of a first mint.

It is also worth saying plainly what this diagram is not: it is one good morning, reconstructed. Stage 0 exists to find out how often mornings like this actually happen — take the coins that did well last week, trace each one back to the post it came from, and check whether we caught it. If the answer is yes often enough, everything after this section is worth building. If it is no, we have found that out in days rather than months.

11. The build plan

Eight stages, in order. Each one has to work before the next one makes any sense — and the first one is not setup. It is the question the whole company rests on, and it comes before we write a line of the product.

The temptation with a plan like this is to start with the visible things: the feed, the page, the buttons. We are deliberately not doing that. Everything visible in this deck sits on top of a single assumption — that our system genuinely finds memeable stories early — and that assumption has never been tested. Every setting in the collector, every cut-off, every filter was an educated guess made by one person in a week. So the first stage tests the guess.

Stage 0 · comes first · days, not months

Prove it actually works

The method is to work backwards. Instead of asking "will our system find good posts?" and waiting to see, we take coins that already did well over the last few days, trace each one back to the viral post it came from, and then check our own records: did we see that post, and how early? The answer already exists in the data we have been collecting. We just have to go and look.

Then the harder half. What did the winners have in common — the kind of story, the platform it started on, the shape of the growth, the language it was in? And critically, the comparison group: posts that went hugely viral and never became anything at all. If the winners and the nothings look identical to us, we have no signal. If they look different, that difference is the product.

STEP ONE
Start at the end
List the coins that actually made money in the last few days. Real outcomes, not our opinions.
STEP TWO
Trace each one home
Find the post that started it. Then check whether it is in our records, and what time we first wrote it down.
STEP THREE
Compare against the nothings
Do the same for posts that went viral and died. The difference between the two piles is the thing we are selling.

The one thing that has to be true

Every feature in this deck assumes our system genuinely finds memeable stories early.

The beautiful narrative page, the coin list, the one-tap create, the comments, the watchlist — all of it is worthless if what we surface is noise. No amount of design rescues a feed full of things nobody would ever make a coin out of. There is no supplier to call and no feature to add that fixes it.

That is why Stage 0 comes first, and why we should not build deep until it is answered. It can be answered in days, not months — the data is already sitting in our own records — and both possible answers are useful.

If the signal is real

We have proof, not a story — traced coins, timestamps, and a measured head start we can put in front of an investor or a first user.

If the signal is not real

We find out before wasting a month, and the misses tell us precisely what to change — more platforms, more languages, different filters.

Either outcome is worth having. The only bad version of this plan is the one where we build stages 1 through 7 first and find out at the end.
