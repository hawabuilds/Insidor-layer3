10. What we store, and what it costs

Eight kinds of thing get written down — six that make the product work and two new ones that let it improve — and the whole machine runs on about six pounds a day. This is the honest accounting: what sits in the database, why each piece has to be there, and where every pound goes.

The database is one central store and the single source of truth. Everything else reads from it. There are six kinds of record that the product itself needs. Narratives are the stories: one entry per viral moment, holding the plain-English two-liner and the pointers to everything else. Posts are the individual social posts that make up a story — the original X post, the TikTok clip someone else put up twelve minutes later. View readings are the important one: every time we re-check a post, we write down the clock time and the view count. That is what draws the climbing line, and it is the one thing we could never buy back later. Nobody sells "what did this post look like twenty minutes ago". If we are not recording it as it happens, it is gone.

Coins are every coin we have tied to a story, with a note of how sure we are about the link. This is a bigger job than it sounds: a search for one meme returned 306 separate tokens, so most of the work is deciding which one is real. Comments are the traders' theses, each stamped with how big the story was at the moment it was written and frozen permanently, so a call made early can never be quietly reframed as a call made late. Running costs are the day's spending on data and AI, written down as we go, so the system knows what it has spent and can stop itself.

Two more are new, and they exist for one reason: the current build makes a few thousand decisions a day and remembers none of them. The decision log records every judgement at the moment it is made — what we were looking at, what we could see at that instant, what we decided, and why. The outcome labels record what actually happened afterwards, settled a week later when the answer is known. Put those two together and you have the only thing that can ever make this system smarter: a list of guesses with the answers attached.

What was broken about the old way of writing things down

The build that exists today cannot learn from itself, and not for want of data — for want of four specific habits. Three of them quietly corrupt the record; the fourth corrupts a number the product depends on. All four are storage problems, which is why they belong in this section rather than a later one.

Four ways the record was spoiled all four are fixed by how we write, not by what we buy
The habit What it did to the record What we do instead
Decisions were never written downa few thousand a day The system judged constantly and kept none of it. There is no way to ask "what did we think of this story at 09:06", because nobody wrote it down. Every judgement is logged as it happens, append-only, with the view of the world it was made from attached.
Numbers had no "as of" timethe growth figure Asking for a post's growth rate later returned a different answer under the same name. The number moved; the question did not. Every stored number carries the moment it was true, and asking about 09:06 always returns the 09:06 answer.
The record length gave away the answersurvivorship Re-checking stopped three hours in, and slow posts were dropped after ten minutes. Winners ended up with long histories and losers with short ones — so any model trained on it learns "posts with lots of readings do well". True, and useless. Same schedule for every post, winner or dud, for the same length of time. A boring history is data too.
Fields were filled with the wrong thingthe TikTok bug TikTok plays were written into a column named views, shares into one named retweets, and quotes were hardcoded to 0 because TikTok has no such thing. Quotes are the signal the product rests on — so every TikTok post reported an honest, invented zero. Each platform's numbers keep their own names, and a thing that platform does not have is stored as blank, never as zero.
Why nothing objected: the old code had no way to describe the shape of a record, so nothing could be checked against it. The new stack does — which is why three of these four become impossible rather than merely discouraged.

The third row is the expensive one. It is the kind of mistake that produces a confident, well-behaved model that has learned nothing except how long we kept watching.

Five streams, and the week that decides them

Five things about the world are perishable: they exist for a moment and then stop being observable at any price. Today only one of the five is being recorded properly. Three are not being recorded at all, and a fourth deletes itself every twelve hours. Everything else in the plan can slip and only cost us time. These cannot: a week not recorded is a week that can never be bought, borrowed or reconstructed.

The scores we keep, and never show

We also calculate internal scores. How fast a post is growing. How memeable the AI judged it. Whether a story has cleared the bar to appear at all. These are stored alongside everything else, they decide which stories reach the feed and in what order, and they are the reason the product works. They are never shown to a user and never sent to their browser. The app's job is to make the judgement so the user does not have to; publishing our workings would hand them the job back. A trader opening the app has about ninety seconds. Give them a heat score of 8.4 and you have not helped them decide — you have handed them a second decision on top of the first.

One question, applied to every field, settles the argument in five seconds. It has a useful side effect: the numbers that pass are exactly the ones a sceptical user can verify elsewhere, so showing them builds credibility. The numbers that fail are the ones we could never prove anyway.

What it costs

About six pounds a day, roughly £181 a month. Reading social posts is by far the biggest line and the only one that scales with how hard we look — two pounds in every three. The AI judging is a little over a pound. The infrastructure — one database, the loops, a watchdog on separate hardware, and the app itself — comes to under a pound a day combined, and the app hosting is genuinely free at this size.

Storage, for scale: a year of readings, decisions and labels is roughly four gigabytes — about fifty pence a month of disk. The irreplaceable part of this business is the cheapest line on the page.

Recording everything is not what makes this expensive. Looking is. Storage is close to free, which is worth saying plainly: the argument for keeping the decision log and the outcome labels never has to be won on cost, only on discipline.

The spending limits are hard, not advisory. Before every request to the data reseller or to the AI, the system checks what it has already spent today against the cap. If the cap is reached, collection pauses until the next day — the machine stops rather than quietly running up a bill overnight while nobody is watching. A runaway loop or a bad day of traffic costs us the rest of that day's stories, which is annoying. It does not cost us a four-figure invoice, which is not.

Above 75%, so only the fastest-growing posts are sent to the AI for the rest of the day. At 100% the requests stop entirely and resume at midnight — and the fact that we stopped is itself written to the decision log, so a thin day is never mistaken later for a quiet one.
Ceiling: £6.00 a day across the two metered suppliers · infrastructure is fixed at about 84p · so the worst possible day is £6.84. Reaching the ceiling pauses collection; it never authorises an overspend.

Two behaviours, not one. At three-quarters spent we get pickier about what is worth paying to judge. At fully spent we stop. Neither needs anyone awake to decide it — and both leave a note explaining the gap.
