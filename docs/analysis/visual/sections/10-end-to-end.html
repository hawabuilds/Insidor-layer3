10. One story, end to end

One morning, minute by minute. This is the Harbor Last Horn story from Part One with the six-part machine from Part Two running underneath it, so you can see exactly which part was doing what, and when.

At 09:00 a Norwegian account called @nordkyst_ferge posts a clip. A retiring ferry captain blows his horn one last time, and every other boat in the harbour blows theirs back. It is a lovely forty seconds of video. Nobody in crypto is looking at it, because as far as crypto is concerned there is nothing there to look at — no coin, no chart, no money moving. Every tool a memecoin trader uses starts working at the moment money starts moving. On this particular morning, that moment is 09:47.

Insidor is looking at 09:04.

Seven moments, drawn to real time. Four of them happen inside nine minutes, which is why the leader lines fan the way they do — the cards are evenly spaced, the moments are not. The cyan band is the product. Everything below the line is the machinery from Part Two, running.

What the user actually did

Almost nothing, which is the point. At around 09:13 they opened Insidor while waiting for a coffee. Near the top of the feed was a card with one sentence on it — “A retiring ferry captain blew his horn one last time, and every boat in the harbour blew back” — a still from the clip, the title Harbor Last Horn, the view count, a tiny line graph still bending upwards, how old it was, and one button reading Create coin, because nobody had made one yet. Seven things, exactly as in Section 2. Who posted it comes on the next screen, when they tap through.

They read the sentence, watched the clip, and decided the horn was funny enough that other people would find it funny too. That took about eighteen minutes of thinking about it and going back to it. At 09:31 they tapped Create. The tap opened pump.fun with a name and ticker already filled in and their wallet already connected through Privy, and $FERRY existed. They were the first person in the world to make a coin out of that video. Sixteen minutes later the rest of crypto Twitter arrived and found a coin that was already there.

What was happening while they did it

At 09:04 the Collector did what it does every couple of minutes: asked our data reseller for posts pulling unusual attention right now. The ferry clip came back with 41,000 views. On its own that number means nothing — it could be a post that exploded in ten minutes or one that crawled there over two days.

So the Measurer put it on a list and looked at it again. At 09:09 the same post read 118,000 views. That second reading is the whole trick: two numbers on the same post five minutes apart is the difference between a story that is still climbing and one that is already over. Nobody will sell us that comparison later. If we are not writing it down as it happens, it is gone.

The Judge then read the post and answered one question — could a person realistically make a memecoin out of this? Most fast-growing posts fail that test: politics, sport, a plane crash. A retiring captain and a harbour full of horns passes it easily, and it comes with a name attached. At 09:12 a second clip of the same moment turned up on TikTok from a different account, and the Grouper tied the two together into one narrative. One person posting is luck. Two independent people posting the same moment is a meme forming.

Every reading, every judgement and both source posts went into the Library as they happened, which is why at 09:13 the App could put the story in front of every user with nothing missing. And it is why, months from now, this morning will be one row in the record that powers Stage 7 — the last stage of the build plan in Section 11, the one that can finally answer “what happened to stories like this one”. It only works if we started writing the rows down today.

  • Every threshold, cut-off and cap in between

The left column is facts about the world — all of it true whether or not Insidor exists. The right column is our reasoning. The user gets the decision. They never get the workings, because working them out is our job, not theirs.

Why the 34 minutes is the company

Axiom, Photon and GMGN are all excellent, and on this morning every one of them was correct and useless. They watch money. There was no money to watch until 09:47. We watch the internet, so we were reading the story at 09:04 and showing it at 09:13. The gap between those two facts is the entire product — and on the days when a coin already exists by the time we surface the story, the same gap simply becomes a better entry price instead of a first mint.

It is also worth saying plainly what this diagram is not: it is one good morning, reconstructed. Stage 0 exists to find out how often mornings like this actually happen — take the coins that did well last week, trace each one back to the post it came from, and check whether we caught it. If the answer is yes often enough, everything after this section is worth building. If it is no, we have found that out in days rather than months.
