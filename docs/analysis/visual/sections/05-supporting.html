05. Trading, watchlist, search and trending

Four supporting features. None of them is the reason someone opens Insidor, but without them the person who spots a story early has to leave to act on it, forgets to come back, buys the wrong one of three hundred coins with the same name, or can only see what we choose to push at them.

Trading — buying built into the page, and the fee said out loud

When a narrative already has a coin, the buy box sits on the same page as the story. No copying a contract address into another app, no second tab, no losing the thing you were reading. We take a small fee on every trade that goes through us, and the whole cost is itemised in the order before you confirm — ours, the exchange's, and the network's, added up into one number and one percentage. Nothing hidden inside the price.

We do not build the trading engine. Routing an order, getting a fair price, handling a failed transaction without losing someone's money is hard, risky, and already solved by firms who do only that. The plan is to route through Axiom or Photon, pending confirmation that they permit it: research found neither publishes a public developer interface, so the integration is not yet proven. The confirmed fallback is Jupiter, which is openly documented and pays us an integrator fee — where Photon charges the user 1% and pays us nothing.

Two facts changed this panel. The first: a search for one meme on pump.fun returned 306 different tokens. Choosing which of them is the real one is a separate problem from spotting the story, and it is the problem that loses people money. So the panel names exactly what you are about to buy — ticker, full contract address, mint time — above the amount box, not buried in a tooltip. The second: among the 1,050 best-performing coins ever minted, the median one peaked near $546,000 and now sits near $3,000. They do not fail to get traction; they get it and give back over 99%. A current market cap shown on its own is therefore a half-truth, so the panel prints the highest market cap the coin has reached and when, right next to today's.

Every number on this panel is a fact you could check somewhere else: price, market cap, the highest market cap it has reached, liquidity, holders, mint time, the last five trades, and each fee by name. The contract address is shown in full because "$FERRY" is not an identity — hundreds of tokens can carry the same three letters, and only the address says which one your money is going into.

Watchlist — the notification is the feature

Saving a narrative is easy to build and easy to ignore. The part that matters is what happens after: if a story you saved gets its first coin, we tell you within seconds. That is the whole retention mechanic. Rather than hoping the user remembers to check back, we open the app for them at the one moment there is something to do.

The measured timing says this alert will usually fire fast. Across coins we could date precisely, the median gap between the post and the first mint was 3.8 minutes, and for coins that went on to clear $10M it was about one minute. Save a story that has no coin and the alert often lands before you have put the phone down. It is worth sending because the mint is not the finish line: no coin that cleared $1M reached its peak within an hour, and the typical gap from post to peak is around six days. A four-minute-old coin is early, not over.

Everything else stays inside the app. If we send noise the user turns notifications off, and you only get to kill this mechanic once.

Search — one box, and it stops duplicates

One field takes three kinds of input: a keyword (“ferry”), a ticker ($FERRY), or a coin address pasted from anywhere. It returns both sides at once — the stories we are tracking and the coins that exist — and its most useful job is unglamorous. A pump.fun search for one popular meme returns 306 distinct tokens carrying that name. So this screen answers two questions with the same list: is there already a coin for this, before I make another one, and which of these is the one people are actually in.

It cannot lean on metadata to do it. Only about 5% of coins above $50K declare the post they came from, so most of the pile is anonymous by construction. The screen therefore shows what each coin itself says: address, mint time, liquidity, and whether it names a source post or not. Coins that declare nothing are still listed — hiding them would be its own kind of lie — but the near-worthless tail is folded into a single line. The median freshly minted coin is worth $28, and a list that presents those as peers of a $799.6K coin is misleading by layout alone.

Trending — ordered by things the user picked

The feed shows what we think is worth seeing right now. Trending lets someone ignore that and look for themselves. It is the release valve for anyone who does not want to be led, and it is deliberately dumb: the only orderings on offer are views and age for stories, and market cap and age for coins. No heat, no score, no "top picks". Every one of those sorts is something the user can verify against the outside world, which means a rank here is never a claim of ours smuggled in as a number.

The coins column carries a second figure: the highest market cap the coin ever reached. Among the best 1,050 coins ever minted the median peaked near $546,000 and now sits near $3,000, so a list of "biggest coins" that hides the peak is a list of survivors mid-collapse presented as winners. Showing both lets someone see at a glance whether they are looking at something still climbing or something already handing money back.

Two lists, two sort controls each, nothing else. Rank 03 is the point of the peak column: by market cap it looks like the third-best coin on the board, and by its own history it has already given back 60%. Both numbers are facts about the world; the user decides what they mean.

All four lean on things we rent rather than build: Privy holds the wallet, Axiom or Photon — or Jupiter — executes the trade, DexScreener supplies price and liquidity, the chain supplies mint times and holders, and the phone delivers the alert. What is ours is the moment each one fires, and the discipline about what reaches the glass. Not one number on these four screens is an Insidor number: views, ages, market caps, peaks, liquidity, holder counts, fees, trades, mint times, contract addresses — every one of them would still be true if Insidor had never been built.
