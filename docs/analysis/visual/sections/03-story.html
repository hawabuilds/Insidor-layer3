03. The story page

The main attraction. One story, one web address: what happened, how far it has travelled, every coin anyone made out of it, and what other traders are saying while they hold or don't hold those coins.

Every story gets its own address — insidor.app/s/harbor-last-horn. A trader pastes that link into a group chat and someone who has never heard of us opens it, understands the thing in one sentence, and can act. No account needed to read it. The page is the marketing.

The layout says what we think matters. Evidence on the left: the clip, the plain-English two-liner, every post we can find, the view count climbing, and where this sits in the clock. Discussion on the right, so a take is never separated from the facts it was made against. The coins across the bottom, full width, because that is the part that decides whether somebody makes or loses money.

What we can honestly claim about time

An earlier version of this page said we show you the story before the coin exists. We measured it, and that is false for every coin worth owning. X post IDs carry their own timestamp, so the gap between a post and the coin made from it is exactly computable: the median is 3.8 minutes, and for coins that later cleared ten million dollars it is 1.1 minutes. Bots do that. Nobody beats them and we should stop saying we will.

What is still open is much larger. Post to peak runs about six days, and of the coins that cleared a million dollars, none peaked inside the first hour. So the page does not sell a head start on the mint. It sells the four hours, or four days, between a coin existing and a coin being finished — and the reason to trust it is the view curve on the left, which we recorded ourselves because nobody sells "what did this post look like twenty minutes ago".

The coin list is the product

One meme spawns a swarm. A pump.fun search for "chill guy" returns 306 distinct tokens. Roughly thirty thousand coins are minted every day, the median one is worth twenty-eight dollars, and in a run of three hundred consecutive mints more than half reused a name already taken — one name was minted twenty-one times. Detecting the story is one problem. Working out which of the 306 is the real one is a completely separate problem, and it is the one that actually costs a user money.

So the list shows four states and never fewer. First is the earliest coin made from this story. Biggest holds the most money right now. They are usually different coins and which one you want depends on how you trade. Matched is everything else we are confident belongs here. Unverified is a coin we suspect belongs and cannot prove — and it gets no buy button at all, not a greyed-out one.

Each row also carries the highest market cap that coin has ever reached, beside the one it has now. Among the thousand best-performing coins ever minted, the median peaked near $546,000 and sits today near $3,000. They do not fail to get traction — they get it and hand back over ninety-nine percent of it. A current market cap shown alone is therefore half a fact, and on this page $HORN's "$1.20M, peak $1.40M" tells a user something that "$1.20M" alone conceals.

Everyone's numbers in their own words

The evidence column lists each post with the platform's own vocabulary — X has views, reposts and quotes; TikTok has plays and shares and no quote-post at all. This is not fussiness. Our own code currently pours TikTok's play count into a column called views, its share count into one called reposts, and writes a zero into quotes because TikTok has no equivalent. Quotes are the signal the whole product rests on — people reproducing a story in their own words — so for every TikTok post that signal silently reads as an honest zero. On this page we print a dash instead, and we never add plays to views to make one bigger number.

The right-hand column, and why nobody else can build it

A take is worthless without knowing when it was made and what the person saying it owned. So both are stamped on at the moment of posting and can never be edited: how big the story was, what the coin was worth, and whether the writer was holding, had sold, or had nothing on the table — read from the connected wallet. Somebody who called this at 340,000 views reads very differently from somebody who turned up at 2.4 million. Neither is disqualifying; both are things you would want to know.

Social apps know what people say. Trading apps know what people own. Insidor is the only place the take and the wallet arrive together, on the same story, at the same second — so a record of who was right, when, and with their own money on it writes itself. A competitor starts with none of that history and cannot buy it later.

What is deliberately not on this page

No score, no heat number, no acceleration figure, no confidence percentage beside $BOAT, no count of the coins we ruled out. The 306 is on screen because pump.fun would return it whether or not we existed; our own arithmetic is not, because it would not. The user gets our judgement — which story, which coins, which one has a button — and never the workings.
