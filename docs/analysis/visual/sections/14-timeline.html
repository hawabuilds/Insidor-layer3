14. The timeline, honestly

This is about four months of work, not two. An earlier version of this plan said eight to nine weeks; that number counted the code and nothing else, and it was off by roughly double. The honest figure is 17 to 18 weeks. What makes four months tolerable is not the total — it is the shape. One date in it matters more than the finish line.

The earlier estimate came from adding up the build steps: write this stage, write that adapter, wire the store. Forty-three developer-days. That arithmetic is roughly right for the code sitting on its own in a fresh folder. It is roughly half the calendar, because it quietly assumes three things that are not true here. It assumes nothing has to be moved from the old system to the new one. It assumes the two never have to run side by side while we check they agree. And it assumes the developer is not simultaneously keeping a live system alive — a system whose record includes a seven-hour silent outage and a heartbeat that stayed dead for two weeks.

Put the missing work back in and the figure is about sixty-seven developer-days. Then divide by a real week rather than an ideal one. One developer, a production system that occasionally demands attention, and a non-engineer reviewer does not produce five clean days a week; three and three-quarters is the honest rate. Sixty-seven divided by 3.75 is 17.9 weeks.

The earlier number

Not in any of these numbers: the models. Those are calendar-bound rather than effort-bound — they need thousands of recorded decisions to exist before anyone can train on them. Month five at the earliest, and only if the decision log ships in week two.

The shape is the good news

Four months is only frightening if you believe the whole thing is worthless until the last day. It is not. The value arrives in a specific order, and the most valuable week is the third one, not the eighteenth.

Five streams of data in this business are perishable. They describe a moment — what a post's view count was at 3:12pm, what we decided about it, what the coin was worth ninety seconds later, how common that phrase had been over the previous fortnight. If you do not write them down as they happen, they are gone. You cannot go back and get them, at any price, ever. Three of those five are not being recorded at all today. A fourth is being recorded and then deleted twelve hours later. That is the asset the company is actually accumulating, and right now most of it is running down a drain.

Read it as two halves, not six dates. The first three weeks stop a permanent loss. The next fifteen convert working code into a product. Only the first half has a deadline set by something other than us.

What moves the number

Two forces act on the four months, and they are not symmetrical. The things that make it longer are mostly decisions; the things that make it shorter are mostly deferrals. That asymmetry is worth internalising: we can lose six weeks by choosing badly in a single afternoon, and we can only claw back one by cutting scope.

What makes it worse
each of these is a choice, not bad luck
  • Deciding the language mid-build. A half-migrated codebase is worse than either end state and appears in no estimate. This has to be settled before the first commit.
  • Building the deck and the design in parallel with the same person writing the code. Two of these three will be late.
  • The old system asking for more than half a day a week. Its history says half a day is optimistic. +1–2 wk
  • Opening the funnel without fixing the cost figures in the same change. The spend guard will throttle at a third of what we can afford, and it will look like a bug in the new system rather than a stale number in the old one.
  • Letting the decision log slip. Every week it moves, the first model moves a week, and nothing recovers it.
What genuinely compresses it
and one saving that is not worth taking
  • One compatibility view over the old store. Removes the riskiest single step and three days with it. −3 d
  • Deferring the second matching method until after TikTok. If simple shared phrases alone group enough stories, the heavier method can wait — and phrases are free, deterministic and language-blind. −4 d
  • Making week 1 a demo, not a chore. Deliberately break something and watch the alarm fire. Week 1 is the week most likely to be skipped, because it produces nothing you can look at — so give it something to look at.
  • Do not cut the TikTok step to save three days. It is the exam. Cutting it means never finding out whether any of this worked, three days from the end.

So the sentence to hold on to is short. Four months, not two, and the earlier number was honest arithmetic on the wrong scope. The finish line is week 18 and it is allowed to move. Week 3 is not, because before week 3 a delay destroys something we can never buy back, and after week 3 a delay only costs us weeks. Every scheduling argument for the rest of this build should be settled by asking which side of week 3 it falls on.
