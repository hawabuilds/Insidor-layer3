15. Where the line sits

Part four is where the two halves of this document meet. Everything before this was the machine; everything after is the screen. Exactly one rule governs what is allowed to cross between them, and it has already caused one version of this deck to be thrown away.

The rule is short. Facts about the world go on screen. Our reasoning never does. A view count is a fact — the post really has 2.4 million views whether or not Insidor was ever built. A market cap is a fact. So is the minute a post went up, the minute a coin was created, the name of the account that posted it, and how many wallets hold the token. All of those existed before we looked and would carry on existing if we shut the servers down tomorrow.

Our reasoning is a different kind of thing. When the system decides a story is worth showing, it is doing arithmetic: comparing a view count against the same post's view count twenty minutes earlier, weighing how many separate accounts are telling the same ferry-captain story, asking Claude how memeable the thing is and getting a number back. Those numbers are real, and they decide the ordering of the entire feed. But they exist only because we invented them. They are our workings, and workings do not go in front of a user.

In the founder's words: "The app's job is to make the judgement so the user doesn't have to. Showing our workings would just make them do our job for us." That is the whole argument. A trader opening Insidor at 09:13 has perhaps ninety seconds. Hand them a heat score of 8.4 and an acceleration ratio of 3.1x and we have not helped — we have added a second decision on top of the first one and asked them to learn our private scale before they can use the product. The correct output of all that arithmetic is not a number. It is an ordered list and one plain sentence.

The same row, twice

Here is the identical story — Harbor Last Horn, post four hours old, three coins already minted from it — rendered both ways. The left is what an earlier draft put in front of a user, and why that draft was rejected. The right is the product.

One thing changed since that draft, and it changed the words rather than the layout. We measured the gap between a post going up and a coin being minted from it: the median is 3.8 minutes, and for the coins that go on to clear ten million it is 1.1 minutes. Bots get there first, always. So the promise can never be "we show you the story before the coin exists." What is still true, and still worth paying for, is that the run takes about six days — the peak comes a median of six days after the post, and no coin above a million ever peaked inside the first hour. Being early to the mint was never the product. The honest claim is therefore: several coins were minted from this story, this is the one that is really about it, and it is still climbing. Every clause of that is a fact. The row carries the first and last of them; the middle one is the story page's whole job, and the row's single button is the door to it.

FIRST, BIGGEST and UNVERIFIED are things a user can act on; 94% is a thing they have to interpret — and since only about one coin in twenty declares the post it came from, that 94% is our inference dressed as measurement. B. A rising line says "still climbing" faster than any percentage and cannot be misread as a promise. C. A disabled button is a taunt — it says we know something and won't tell you; nothing, plus a link out, is honest. D. The two-liner is not a summary of the reasoning, it is the reasoning, delivered. E. Our TikTok parser writes play counts into a column called views; a mislabelled fact is worse on screen than an omitted one. F. The order is our loudest opinion, so it is expressed as order and nothing else — no positions, no totals, no count of what we filtered out.

The test

Any new screen, any new field, any argument about whether something belongs — this settles it in about five seconds, and anyone on the team can apply it without asking permission.

Before you put a number on screen, ask
Would this number still be true if Insidor did not exist?
Yes → it can be shown
2.4M views — the post has them either way
$799.6K — on-chain, public, checkable
minted 22m ago — the block says so
1,204 holders — anyone can count them
No → it stays behind the scenes
meme_score 7 — we invented the scale
accel 3.1x — needs our own history to exist
match 87% — our uncertainty, not theirs
rank ▲4 — a fact about our list, not the world
Second question, for anything that passes: is it still the quantity its label claims? A TikTok play count printed under the word "views" passes the first test and fails this one.

One question, applied to every field, plus a second one for anything that survives it. Useful side effect: the numbers that pass are exactly the numbers a sceptical user can verify somewhere else, so showing them buys credibility. The numbers that fail are the ones we could never prove anyway.

There is a discipline hidden in this that is worth naming. A product that never shows its scores cannot hide behind them. If the ordering is wrong, the user sees a bad story at the top and nothing else — no 8.4 to argue with, no gate to blame. The line does not only protect the user from our arithmetic. It removes every excuse for the arithmetic being poor.
