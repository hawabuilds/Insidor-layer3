08. What we store, and what it costs

Six kinds of thing get written down, and the whole machine runs on about five pounds a day. This section is the honest accounting: what sits in the database, why each piece has to be there, and where every pound goes.

The database — the Library, part five of the machine — is one central store and the single source of truth. Everything else reads from it. There are six kinds of record. Narratives are the stories themselves: one entry per viral moment, holding the plain-English two-liner and the pointers to everything else. Posts are the individual social posts that make up a story — the original X post, the TikTok clip someone else put up twenty minutes later. View readings are the important one: every single time the Measurer re-checked a post, we write down the clock time and the view count. That is what draws the climbing line, and it is the one thing we could never buy back later. Nobody sells "what did this post look like twenty minutes ago". If we are not recording it as it happens, it is gone.

Coins are every coin we have tied to a story, with a note of how sure we are about the link — some are obvious, some are a guess, and we keep the difference. Comments are the traders' theses, each one stamped with how big the story was at the moment it was written, frozen permanently so a call made early can never be quietly reframed as a call made late. Running costs are the day's spending on data and AI, written down as we go, so the system knows what it has spent and can stop itself.

The scores we keep, and never show

We also calculate internal scores. How fast a post is growing. How memeable the AI thinks a story is. Whether a story has cleared the bar to appear at all. These are stored alongside everything else, and they are what decides which stories reach the feed and in what order — they are the reason the product works. They are never shown to a user and never sent to their browser. The app's job is to make the judgement so the user does not have to. Publishing our workings would just hand them the job back.

The test: if a number would still be true with Insidor switched off, it can go on screen. If it only exists because we calculated it, it does not.

What it costs

About £5 a day, roughly £150 a month. Reading social posts is by far the biggest line and the only one that scales with how hard we look — four pounds in five. The AI judging is a pound. The database and the hosting are genuinely free at this size, and stay free until we are considerably busier than we are on day one.

The spending limits are hard, not advisory. Before every request to the data reseller or to the AI, the system checks what it has already spent today against the cap. If the cap is reached, collection pauses until the next day — the machine stops rather than quietly running up a bill overnight while nobody is watching. A runaway loop or a bad day of traffic costs us the rest of that day's stories, which is annoying. It does not cost us a four-figure invoice, which is not.
