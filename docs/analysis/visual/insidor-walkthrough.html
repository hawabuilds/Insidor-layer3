01. What Insidor is

Memecoins are made out of things going viral. Every tool a trader uses today starts at the money — it can only show a coin once the coin exists and buying has begun. Insidor starts at the internet instead: we watch the posts, and when coins appear off one of them we tell you which one is the real one, while there is still a run left to catch.

What we are no longer claiming

An earlier version of this deck said we would show you the story before the coin exists. We measured it, and that is not true for any coin worth owning. Post IDs on X carry their own timestamp, so the gap between a post and the coin minted from it can be computed exactly. The median is 3.8 minutes. For coins that went on to clear a million dollars it is 1.1 minutes, and 88% of them were minted inside the first hour. The slower the mint, the worse the coin did. Chill Guy's famous thirteen-month gap turned out to be an old tweet dug up and re-minted years later, and every case like it topped out between $159K and $1.8M.

So the first two minutes belong to bots and always will. There is no product in racing them, and pretending otherwise would have sent us building the wrong thing for four months.

What is actually still open

Two measurements say the opportunity moved rather than disappeared. First, a story does not produce one coin — it produces a crowd of them. Searching pump.fun for "chill guy" returns 306 separate tokens. One of those made people money and 305 did not, and nothing on the coin itself tells you which. Only about 5% of coins above $50K even declare the post they came from, so you cannot look it up.

Second, the run is long. From the original post to the coin's peak is about six days, and of every coin that cleared a million dollars, none peaked inside the first hour. The mint is over in minutes; the move is not. That is the whole shape of the business: you cannot be first to the coin, but you can be early to the right one, and "early" now means hours rather than seconds.

Why it is defensible

Being right about which coin is real is not a feature anyone can copy in a fortnight, because it rests on two things that only get bigger while the product runs.

COMPOUND #1 · THE COMMUNITY
Their thesis history lives here

Traders argue on each story about which of the coins is the real one, in public, under their own name. Over months that becomes a track record you cannot fake and cannot take with you.

People do not walk away from a reputation they spent a year building. The writing is the lock-in, and we are not the ones who have to produce it.

COMPOUND #2 · THE RECORD
Which posts became coins that made money

Every story we surface we keep: what the post looked like as it climbed, which coins appeared off it, and how each one ended. Nobody sells this data. You cannot go back and ask what a post looked like twenty minutes ago — if it was not recorded live, it is gone forever.

A competitor can copy the screen in a fortnight. They cannot copy a year of yesterdays.

Who this is for

THE TRADER
Wants the right coin, not the fast one

Already reads charts, already has Axiom open. What they cannot get anywhere is a reason to pick one of the fourteen coins named after the same video an hour after it was posted.

THE CREATOR
Wants to mint off a meme

Finding the funny thing early is their whole job, and today it means scrolling all day and hoping. Here the stories arrive already sorted, with what has been minted off each.

THE NEWCOMER
Saw it on TikTok, wants in

No wallet, no jargon, no idea what a bonding curve is. They get the story in one sentence, one coin recommended over the copies, and a button that works.

Three people, one screen. The trader reads it for the pick, the creator for the raw material, the newcomer for the sentence at the top. Nothing on the page is written for an insider only.

02. The feed

The home screen. One list of the stories going viral right now, best one at the top, each explained in two lines of plain English so you never have to watch the video to know what it is. Seven things per row. Nothing about how we picked them.

Every other tool a trader opens — Axiom, Photon, GMGN — is a list of coins. Insidor's home screen is a list of stories. Some of them have a coin, some have three, some have none. The list is what the coins are about, which is the thing nobody else shows you and the only thing that determines whether a coin keeps going.

The promise we had to correct

An earlier version of this deck said the feed shows you the story before the coin exists. We measured it, and that is not true for the coins that make money. Every X post carries its own timestamp inside its ID, so the gap between a post going up and a coin being minted from it can be computed exactly rather than guessed. The median gap is 3.8 minutes. For coins that went on to clear $10M it is 1.1 minutes, and 88% of them were minted inside the first hour. Automated minters are watching the same firehose and they will always be first. That race is lost and it was never worth entering.

✗  What we used to say

"We show you the story before the coin exists."

Measured and false where it matters. Median post-to-mint is 3.8 minutes; for coins that reached $10M it is 1.1 minutes. Bots mint within a few minutes of a post going up. The one famous slow case — a meme minted thirteen months later — turned out to be the shape of the losing regime: every coin we found minted more than thirty days after its post was an old tweet dug up and re-used, and none of them got far.

✓  What we can actually say

"Of the things minted in the last hour, this is the real story — and it is still climbing."

A post takes about six days to reach its own peak, and zero percent of coins that cleared $1M peaked inside their first hour. So the mint is the starting gun, not the finish. Minutes one to ten belong to machines. Hour one to day six is where a human with a clear read on the story can still be early, and that is the window the feed is built for.

The two lines are the product

The most important text in the whole app is the sentence under each title. "A retiring ferry captain blew his horn one last time, and every boat in the harbour blew back." Read that and you already know whether it is worth a coin — you can feel whether it is funny, whether it has a name in it, whether people will repeat it. So it is set larger and brighter than the title above it. It is not a subtitle. It is the row.

Everything else on the row is a fact about the world that would be true whether or not Insidor existed: how many people have seen it, whether the line is still going up, how old it is, and what you can do about it. Seven things, no eighth. The order of the list is where all our work goes, and the order is the only place it shows.

The still frame from the video or image. It is how you recognise a story you have already seen scrolling elsewhere, in a fifth of a second, before reading anything.

2

"Harbor Last Horn." A short handle so the story can be referred to, searched for, and pasted into a group chat. Deliberately set smaller than the sentence below it.

3

The point of the entire screen. You should understand the story without watching the video. Largest, brightest text in the row — it is what you actually judge the trade on.

The headline number, big and unambiguous. A true fact about the world: this many people have seen it. Not our estimate of anything, just the count.

5

The view count over the last hour. Answers the one question a single number cannot: is it still going up, or is it over? Cyan and rising, or grey and flat.

6
How old it is

Four hours. Six hours. Read against a six-day arc it is the whole timing story — 850K in two hours is a different animal from 850K in two days.

7

Create coin if nobody has made one, Buy $TICKER if one is clearly the real one, 3 coins → if several exist and picking is its own job. One row, one next move.

What we removed, and where it went

An earlier version of this screen showed the row as a trading terminal: columns headed HEAT, ACCEL, ORG and MEME, a twenty-minute chart of view-rate change, and a footer counting how many candidates our filters had thrown away. Every number on it was real. It still had to go. The test is simple — would this number still be true if Insidor did not exist? Views, age, who posted it: yes. Heat, acceleration, meme score, how many we filtered: no. Those are our opinion wearing a number's clothes, and putting them on screen hands the judgement back to the person we built the company to make it for.

03. The story page

The main attraction. One story, one web address: what happened, how far it has travelled, every coin anyone made out of it, and what other traders are saying while they hold or don't hold those coins.

Every story gets its own address — insidor.app/s/harbor-last-horn. A trader pastes that link into a group chat and someone who has never heard of us opens it, understands the thing in one sentence, and can act. No account needed to read it. The page is the marketing.

The layout says what we think matters. Evidence on the left: the clip, the plain-English two-liner, every post we can find, the view count climbing, and where this sits in the clock. Discussion on the right, so a take is never separated from the facts it was made against. The coins across the bottom, full width, because that is the part that decides whether somebody makes or loses money.

One meme spawns a swarm. A pump.fun search for "chill guy" returns 306 distinct tokens. Everyone's numbers in their own words

The evidence column lists each post with the platform's own vocabulary — X has views, reposts and quotes; TikTok has plays and shares and no quote-post at all. This is not fussiness. Our own code currently pours TikTok's play count into a column called views, its share count into one called reposts, and writes a zero into quotes because TikTok has no equivalent. Quotes are the signal the whole product rests on — people reproducing a story in their own words — so for every TikTok post that signal silently reads as an honest zero. On this page we print a dash instead, and we never add plays to views to make one bigger number.

The right-hand column, and why nobody else can build it

A take is worthless without knowing when it was made and what the person saying it owned. So both are stamped on at the moment of posting and can never be edited: how big the story was, what the coin was worth, and whether the writer was holding, had sold, or had nothing on the table — read from the connected wallet. Somebody who called this at 340,000 views reads very differently from somebody who turned up at 2.4 million. Neither is disqualifying; both are things you would want to know.

Social apps know what people say. Trading apps know what people own. Insidor is the only place the take and the wallet arrive together, on the same story, at the same second — so a record of who was right, when, and with their own money on it writes itself. A competitor starts with none of that history and cannot buy it later.

The user gets our judgement — which story, which coins, which one has a button — and never the workings.

04. Create a coin

An earlier version of this section promised the user would be first. The timing data says that is false for every coin worth being first on. So this screen keeps the same shape and loses the boast: it is the smaller of the two doors, it tells the truth about the odds on the screen itself, and — unlike the build we have today — it actually creates a coin.

The claim we used to make, and why it is dead

X post IDs contain their own timestamp, so the gap between a post going up and a coin being minted from it is computable, not guessable. The median gap is 3.8 minutes, and it shortens as the coin does better: 1.2 minutes above a $1M peak, 1.1 minutes above $10M, with 88% of those minted inside the first hour. Bots watch the same firehose and are faster than any interface with a human in it. Chill Guy — the famous thirteen-month gap — was an archived tweet re-minted years later, and every case like it peaked between $159K and $1.8M. The slow regime is the losing regime.

The second half of that measurement is the good news. Post-to-peak is about six days, and zero percent of coins clearing $1M peaked inside their first hour. The mint window shuts in minutes; the price window stays open for days. That is why buying (section 03) is the main door and this is the side door.

Two different clocks, drawn on one line. Creating is a race we lose in the first ninety seconds. Trading is a race that runs for a week. The product should be weighted accordingly.

06. How we find viral posts

About half a billion posts go up on X every day. We can pay to look at roughly thirty thousand of them, and to look twice at about nine thousand. Everything in this section is about how the machine chooses which nine thousand — and why waiting for a post to get big is the one strategy guaranteed to be too late.

Reading a post costs money — fifteen hundredths of a cent. That sounds like nothing until you notice that a post worth following has to be read eight or nine times, because you cannot tell whether something is speeding up from a single look. So a budget of thirty-three thousand reads a day does not buy thirty-three thousand posts. It buys about nine thousand watched properly, out of half a billion. One post in fifty thousand. Every other post is a decision made without ever seeing what happened next.

Which is the honest way to describe this part of Insidor: not a detector, a chooser. The funnel below is all of it, and the brutal steps are the first two, not the last two.

What survives each step — a day in the life of the machine

Each step has to throw away at least 90% of what it received, or it is not a step — it is a rename. The one place the current build fails that test is grouping: 1,200 posts in, 1,289 stories out. That is section 08's problem.

Why "show me posts over 100K views" is structurally too late

The obvious build is a search: give me every post above a hundred thousand views. It is obvious, it is cheap, and it cannot work — for three separate reasons, any one of which is fatal.

A threshold is a queue. The number is public, anyone can run that query, and the people running it fastest are not people. Using the timestamp X hides inside every post's ID, we measured the gap between a post going up and a coin being minted from it: median 3.8 minutes, and it gets shorter the better the coin does — 1.2 minutes for coins that peaked above a million dollars. A post takes most of an hour to reach a hundred thousand views. By the time the threshold fires, the coin has existed for fifty minutes.

A threshold is a level, and a level is history. Five hundred thousand views is a statement about what already happened, not about whether anything is still happening. Two posts can sit on the same number for opposite reasons, and the chart below is that fact drawn out.

A threshold means different things to different accounts. A hundred thousand views on an account with two million followers is a quiet afternoon. The same number on an account with four thousand followers is a structural event — it means the post left the building.

The mechanic everything else hangs off

Virality begins at the moment a post escapes its author's own followers. Not when it gets big — when the engagement starts arriving from strangers.

Everything before that point is an account talking to the audience it already has, which is predictable from follower count alone. The first quote or repost from somebody with no connection to the author is the earliest evidence that anything unusual is happening, and it arrives minutes before the view count says anything at all.

This is not a metaphor about how the internet feels. It is how X is built. Posts reach you through two pipes: one seeded from the accounts you follow, and one that searches the whole site for things it thinks you would like regardless of who wrote them. The second pipe is where reach comes from, and it does not wait for a post to accumulate engagement before it starts distributing. Being early is technically possible rather than merely aspirational.

The crossing — the same post, thirty minutes apart

X does not publish a follower / non-follower split on its view count — it is one aggregate number. So the crossing is measured by proxy, from things that do ship free with every post.

Four proxies do most of the work, and the important thing about all four is that they cost nothing extra. They are already sitting in the data we paid for when we read the post the first time.

The snapshot grid

Once a post is admitted, we go back and look at it again on a fixed schedule — dense at the start, thinning out as it ages, because all the interesting change happens in the first half hour.

When we look again — eight readings in the first eighty minutes

A fixed 2% of everything that arrives runs this full grid regardless of score. That slice is expensive and it is not optional: without it, how long a post's history is depends on how well the post did, and anything we later learn from that history is really just learning "posts we watched a lot did well."

Two readings give a speed, and speed normalises in a way a level cannot: divide by the author's own recent baseline and by what is normal for that platform at that hour, and a tiny account and a huge one land on the same scale. One caution. An author's baseline is under that author's control — push it down with filler posts, then buy engagement on the one that matters. So the per-author comparison only ever re-orders candidates that have already cleared a hard absolute floor. A gate an attacker can open by buying a hundred likes is worse than no gate.

Three readings would tell us whether it is speeding up or slowing down, but three readings is fourteen minutes, and by fourteen minutes the coins have been minted for ten of them and the argument about which one is real is already running without us. So instead we keep two running averages of the same counter — one that reacts in twenty minutes, one over six hours — and compare them. The ratio stands in for the curve, and it exists at the second reading. Five minutes earlier, on a product whose whole job is naming the real coin while most of the six-day run is still ahead of it.

A threshold search would have returned both, and it would have returned Post A first, because Post A crossed the line first.

What this buys, stated honestly

This produces candidates, not certainties. It will not beat a bot to a mint — nothing will, and we stopped trying.

What it buys is position. Coins that clear a million dollars take about six days to peak, and none of them peaked inside the first hour. So the winnable claim is not "we saw this before the coin existed". It is "of the things minted in the last hour, this is the real narrative, and it is still climbing." Everything on this page exists to make that second sentence true.

The published ceiling on this kind of prediction is a real ceiling: once you control for how early you are — exactly our situation, since every candidate is already fast — accuracy across four independent research domains falls below 65%. We will miss things. The 2% slice we track regardless of score is how we find out what we missed, and whether the fault was the chooser or the judge.

07. How posts become stories

One person posting a video of a Norwegian ferry captain is luck. Twenty unrelated people posting their own version of it, on two platforms, inside an hour, is a meme forming. The part of the system that notices the difference is called the Grouper, and it is not a tidying step that bundles duplicates together neatly. It is the detector.

Everything upstream of this operates on one post at a time, and one post at a time cannot tell a hit from an accident. Plenty of single posts get eight million views. Almost none become coins that clear a million dollars. What separates them is whether other people started making their own versions.

The hypothesis this whole product rests on

Memes get copied. News gets consumed. A plane crash gets millions of views and nobody makes their own plane crash — people watch it, react, move on. A ferry captain blowing his horn one last time gets re-cut, screenshotted, drawn and parodied by people who have never heard of each other. A view counter cannot tell those apart; both are a big number going up. Counting how many independent people made their own version tells them apart instantly.

What it does today, stated plainly

The failure

982 of the 1,079 stories that have any posts in them contain exactly one post, by exactly one author. That is 91%.

The product's premise is that people are making their own versions. The Grouper, as written, cannot see a second version.

The cause is a single file. What the code calls embeddings is not embeddings — it is a word-count map. "Captain", "ferry" and "horn" have to literally appear in both posts for them to be joined. A Norwegian post about the same captain shares no words. A TikTok with no caption shares nothing at all. And "every boat answered him" shares nothing with "the whole harbour blew back", though any human reads them as the same moment.

Worse, there is redundancy nobody had noticed. There are three matching passes, and the third is the second with different weights: any pair the keyword pass accepts, the word-count pass has already accepted. It contributes zero additional matches, at the cost of a third of the code. Meanwhile the word-count pass, with no sense of which words are rare and no penalty for length, invents joins — "Behind-the-scenes of Adwoa Aboah's…" and "Behind the scenes with the champs" score 0.436, over the 0.42 line, and become one story.

The fix, and the four small things that must ship with it

Real sentence meaning, in place of word counting, is the highest-leverage single change in the entire system. Not a research project — a well-understood bought capability at about eleven cents a day. But dropping it in alone would make things worse, and four small things have to go with it.

  1. Stop only reading English. The collector asks X for English posts only. No meaning model can group across languages while the front door is English-only. Moo Deng's earliest signal was a Thai post at 4.6M views, seven to eleven days before the English wave.
  2. Do not reuse the old cut-off. The 0.42 line was tuned for word counting. Sentence-meaning scores sit on a different scale, where unrelated sentences routinely clear 0.42. Leaving the number would merge nearly everything into one story.
  3. Ignore the words that are always there. One story swallowed 39 unrelated posts because they all mentioned $SOL. A list of 45 such words already exists in the codebase, imported by three other modules and by nothing in the grouping path. One import line — the highest value per line of code in the repository.
  4. Weight words by how new they are, not how rare. The standard technique punishes any word that suddenly appears everywhere, which is exactly what a brand-new ticker does at the moment it matters. The right measure is how many of the last fourteen days a word has been common: $SOL every day, so it counts for nothing; yesterday's ticker counts for almost everything. That history only accrues forward — if the daily table is not being written today, this feature does not exist in November.
A related bug worth naming here

Quotes — someone reposting with their own commentary — is the clearest reproduction signal we have, and the one the product rests on.

The TikTok reader hardcodes quotes = 0, because TikTok has no quote feature. So for every TikTok post the core replication signal reads as an honest zero, and nothing objects. Cross-platform copying is currently invisible in one direction.

One cheap experiment should run first: a column already exists that records which pass made each match, and it is empty on every row. Fixing that one write answers which pass earns its keep. It is an afternoon.

Then rules first, a model later. Once the fingerprint tier runs and the meaning tier sits behind a calibrated line, the way to improve it is three hours of hand-labelling — three hundred pairs, "same real-world moment, yes or no" — and fitting a small model to the six match signals in place of hand-set weights. No research, no new service: fingerprints and meaning vectors are both columns in the Postgres database that already runs.

08. How stories become coins

A story is not a coin. One meme can spawn hundreds of them, only a few are real, and almost none of them say where they came from. This is the part of the product where being wrong costs a user actual money — so it is built to say "I don't know" more often than it says anything else.

Everything before this was about reading the world. This is the one place we act on that reading. A user sees a story, sees a coin next to it, and presses Buy. If the coin is the wrong one, we did not make a bad recommendation — we spent their money on a stranger's token.

Two measured facts define the problem. First, a pump.fun search for "chill guy" returns 306 distinct tokens — one meme, three hundred coins, one or two of which people actually traded. Second, only about 5% of coins that ever cleared $50,000 declare the post they came from. The metadata field exists; it is nearly always empty, and when it is filled it was filled by whoever launched the coin, so it is advertising, not evidence. We cannot ask the coin where it came from. We have to work it out.

What the current build does, and the two coins it bought into

There is no check that the coin was created after the post. There is no confidence score, and the database column is a plain true/false — a boolean cannot abstain.

The gates, cheapest first — and time is the cheapest of all

The fix is not a smarter model. It is putting the checks in the right order, and the right order is by what each check costs to run. A subtraction of two timestamps is free. Reading a token's transfer rules is one cheap call. Asking what it would actually cost to buy is a paid, rate-limited call. So we ask them in that order and stop at the first refusal, which means the overwhelming majority of candidates are eliminated without spending anything.

Time goes first for a second reason beyond cost: it is the only check that is logically decisive. A coin minted before the post cannot have been created by the post. That is not a probability, it is an impossibility, and it is exactly the check that would have killed both failures above. It also inverts how candidates are found. Today the code searches by ticker and then filters; the rebuild retrieves by time — every coin minted in the window that starts at the story's earliest post — and treats the ticker as one scoring signal over that set, never as the way the set is fetched.

The liquidity test that deleted our own market

This correction changes what "can this be bought" even means. The old build refused any coin whose liquidity was not greater than zero. That sounds prudent; it is catastrophic. A brand-new coin on a bonding curve has no liquidity figure at all — not zero, absent, because that kind of market has no two-sided reserve for the number to describe. Measured live, every bonding-curve pair returned no liquidity object, and the code turned "absent" into "zero" and rejected them. So the filter removed all the new coins, which is the entire population this product exists to serve, and kept only the old survivors — a second, quieter reason the matcher kept landing on year-old tokens. The replacement test means what we want: can we get a real quote, at a real price, right now.

Once the survivors are scored on ticker, wording, image and declared links, there is one more rule, and it is the one usually missing from systems like this. We do not check whether the best candidate scores well. We check whether it beats the second-best by a clear margin. 09. How the system learns

The idea is smaller than it sounds. Write down what the system saw at the moment it made a call. Days later, write down what actually happened. Put the two lists side by side and you have taught it something. That is the whole machine-learning plan, and it starts working before there is any model at all.

Right now the system makes a few thousand calls a day — this post is worth watching, that one is not, these ten posts are the same story — and remembers none of them. Tomorrow it makes a few thousand more, no wiser than yesterday. The reason is not that it lacks a model. It is that it never wrote anything down. A model built on nothing is still nothing.

The awkward gap: minutes to decide, days to find out

The decision has to happen fast. A post goes up and a coin appears a median of 3.8 minutes later. Whether that coin was worth anything takes about six days to know — that is when the typical winner peaks. Every call is made on minute-old evidence and graded on week-old evidence. In that week the post keeps moving and the replies fill up, so anything not captured at the moment of the decision is not merely harder to get later. It is a different thing wearing the same name.

Two lists, one join

So: two tables. The first is written the instant a call is made and never touched again. The second is written days later by a job that goes and checks. They share one key.

Holding a few slots back on purpose

There is a trap in a system that only ever acts on its own best guess: it only ever finds out about the things it already liked. Everything it declined to look at stays permanently unknown, so the record slowly becomes a portrait of its own habits. The fix is to give away a small, deliberate, budgeted amount of the decision to chance.

Two kinds of held-back slot — one costs feed quality, one costs almost nothing

The two random board slots cost feed quality and buy honest evidence about the boundary. The shadow lane costs about two per cent of the watching budget, costs zero feed quality, and is the only thing that ever measures what the filters threw away.

The shadow lane is the one almost nobody builds, and it answers a question no other measurement can. Suppose we are missing winners. Is that because the ranking is bad, or because the front gate never let those posts through at all? From inside the normal path the two are indistinguishable, since everything the gate refused is simply absent from the record. Compare the shadow lane's hit rate against the gated one and the answer falls out: if the shadow lane is full of winners the gated path never saw, the gate is the problem, and no amount of model work will fix it.

The two random board slots have a price, and it should be written down with a number attached or it gets quietly deleted the first bad week. On a twenty-row board it costs roughly 0.6 winners a day not surfaced. That is the fee for being able to discover something unlike anything the system has seen before, and somebody should own it. If money ever forces a cut, cut the random slots and keep the shadow lane: random slots can be switched back on, but a missing shadow lane leaves a permanent hole no future work can fill.

One related rule follows from the same worry and is enforceable in code: nothing may score a story by how much it resembles a past winner. That is the fastest route to a system that finds only derivatives, which is worthless in a market whose entire product is the thing nobody predicted. One number polices it forever — the hit rate on the winners least like anything seen before. If that collapses, the system has become a copy machine no matter how good the headline looks.

The loop, end to end

The loop — it closes in about a week, and then it never stops

Steps 3 and 6 are the two that do not exist today. Everything else in the loop is either already running or is a week of work. The gap between them — the six days — is why they cannot be written retrospectively.

The one line that matters for scheduling

Every day this is not recording is a day of training data that can never be bought, rebuilt, or backfilled.

The rest of the build can slip and cost time. This one slips and costs the asset — which is why week 3 is drawn as a point of no regret rather than a milestone. It is also the least glamorous work in the plan: it ships nothing a user can see, and it is the only part that makes the company harder to copy in a year than it is today.

One last thing, because it is the rule the whole deck exists to enforce: none of this ever appears on a screen. The two lists live in a part of the database the public app is structurally incapable of reading — not "we remembered not to expose it", but a separate compartment the public interface has never been told about. That matters more here than anywhere, because these tables hold every threshold, every held-back slot and every number the system looked at, which together is a complete recipe for gaming the gates. The user sees a story, a view count, an age, and a coin. What we thought about it stays inside.

10. What we store, and what it costs

Eight kinds of thing get written down — six that make the product work and two new ones that let it improve — and the whole machine runs on about six pounds a day. This is the honest accounting: what sits in the database, why each piece has to be there, and where every pound goes.

The database is one central store and the single source of truth. Everything else reads from it. There are six kinds of record that the product itself needs. Narratives are the stories: one entry per viral moment, holding the plain-English two-liner and the pointers to everything else. Posts are the individual social posts that make up a story — the original X post, the TikTok clip someone else put up twelve minutes later. View readings are the important one: every time we re-check a post, we write down the clock time and the view count. That is what draws the climbing line, and it is the one thing we could never buy back later. Nobody sells "what did this post look like twenty minutes ago". If we are not recording it as it happens, it is gone.

Coins are every coin we have tied to a story, with a note of how sure we are about the link. This is a bigger job than it sounds: a search for one meme returned 306 separate tokens, so most of the work is deciding which one is real. Comments are the traders' theses, each stamped with how big the story was at the moment it was written and frozen permanently, so a call made early can never be quietly reframed as a call made late. Running costs are the day's spending on data and AI, written down as we go, so the system knows what it has spent and can stop itself.

Two more are new, and they exist for one reason: the current build makes a few thousand decisions a day and remembers none of them. The decision log records every judgement at the moment it is made — what we were looking at, what we could see at that instant, what we decided, and why. The outcome labels record what actually happened afterwards, settled a week later when the answer is known. Put those two together and you have the only thing that can ever make this system smarter: a list of guesses with the answers attached.

What types actually buy

A contract is a plug shape. Right now the system has plug shapes drawn on paper next to each socket, and everyone agrees they should match. Occasionally someone plugs in the wrong thing, it fits physically, and the appliance runs slightly wrong for six months. Types make the plug shapes physical. The wrong adapter does not fit. You find out while you are holding it, not in a report three weeks later.

Under the new shape, an adapter has to declare what its platform cannot count. TikTok's adapter says "reproduction: absent" — not zero, absent — and core switches to deriving copies from the fingerprint index instead. You cannot ship an adapter that quietly claims a counter it does not have, because the declaration is required to build the object at all. 12. Where the code lives

You told us you could not find the logic in the build we have. That is not a documentation problem, and no README fixes it. The folder names have to answer the question on their own — so the shape below is designed around one measurable test: how long it takes a person who has never seen this repository to open the right file.

The current build fails that test for a specific reason. The place where a post is judged worth coining is worker/adapters/anthropic/meme-score.js — a file inside a folder named after a vendor. It reaches back into worker/score/lib/meme-gate and worker/score/lib/nameability, and it writes the number 0.4 out as a final verdict at four separate line numbers. So the product decision lives in the API-client folder, the thresholds live somewhere else again, and there is nowhere you could have looked that would have been wrong to look. Elsewhere in the same tree, a vendor file and a policy file import each other in a circle, and somebody has already hidden one of those imports inside a function to stop Node complaining. That is not sloppiness. It is the absence of a boundary.

Three layers, and only one of them thinks

The replacement has exactly three layers, and the whole design is one sentence: logic never knows who it is talking to, and vendors never know what the logic decides.

Adapters sit at the edge. Every outside thing — X, TikTok, Reddit, the Solana chain, pump.fun, Dexscreener, Jupiter, Rugcheck, Claude, the embedding model — gets exactly one folder, and that folder's only job is translation: take whatever that service hands back and restate it in our words. An adapter holds no thresholds, makes no product decisions, and contains no SQL. Adding a platform means writing one folder and one line in a registry, instead of forking the pipeline the way TikTok was forked into a second 340-line copy today.

The core holds all the logic, and is pure. No network, no clock, no database, no random numbers, no npm dependencies, and — this is the one that matters most — no platform names anywhere in it. Not views, not retweets, not playCount. Those are X's words and TikTok's words, and the moment they appear in the core, every future platform has to pretend it has them. That is precisely how TikTok's share count ended up in a column called retweets and how quotes — the reproduction signal the whole product rests on — became an honest-looking zero. Pure also means the core is the only part of the system you can replay: hand it yesterday's recorded inputs and it produces yesterday's answer, every time, with no key and no internet.

The record sits underneath. One database, one folder that is allowed to contain SQL, and every decision the core made written down as it happens. And one more wall past that: the app reads a separate, deliberately duplicated wire vocabulary that has no internal field in it, so it is not that the app should not show a score — it structurally cannot obtain one.

Reading down: the outside world comes in on the left of the top band, gets restated in our vocabulary, is judged once in the middle, is written down, and only then is translated back out. The rail down the left is the set of words all four layers agree on. Nothing skips a band.

A tool walks every import in the repository against an allow-list — not a list of forbidden pairs, which always misses one, but a list of what each package may reach. It also fails on circles, the exact defect in the build today.

Layer 4
the word check

A leak usually arrives as a field name, not an import. So a script fails the build on any platform, chain or vendor word inside core or contracts — views, retweet, playCount, pump.fun, solana, claude.

The test: how many seconds to the right file

Here is the actual measure of whether any of this worked. A developer — or you, or a person hired next year — wants to change how a post is judged worth making a coin out of. No README, no search, no asking anybody.

0s
It is a judgement.

Judgements live in one place and that place is called core/. Adapters cannot make one; the store cannot make one; the app cannot see one.

3s
The folders are the verbs.

admit · track · detect · group · qualify · resolve · rank. "Is there a coinable thing here" is qualify. There is nowhere else it could be.

7s
Every stage has a stage.ts.

Not index.ts, not qualifier.ts, not helpers.ts. The rule holds in all seven folders, so it needs no memory.

11s
Open it.

core/qualify/stage.ts — about 120 lines, no network calls, no numbers in it. The numbers it uses are named, and they are all in contracts/policy.ts.

Eleven seconds, three decisions, zero searches. The same walk finds every other judgement in the system, because the tree has exactly one shape.

And two things follow that are worth more than the eleven seconds. Because the file has no numbers in it, changing a threshold is a one-line edit in a file that lists every threshold in the product side by side — you can see what else you are near. Because the file has no network calls in it, you can run the change against every decision the system has already made and see what would have changed, before shipping it. Neither is possible today.

The same question, in the build we have

The product decision is inside the folder named after the model provider. The thresholds it applies live in a third place. Nothing in the folder names points here.

Meanwhile a vendor file and a policy file import each other in a circle, and one of those imports has been hidden inside a function to keep Node from noticing.

The same question, after
core/qualify/stage.ts ├─ imports contracts ← types and thresholds only ├─ no fetch · no clock · no database · no vendor └─ every number named, defined in contracts/policy.ts

The model's opinion arrives as an ordinary field, handed in by the service that called it. If it is missing, the stage abstains with a named reason rather than guessing.

Which means the same file can be replayed over months of past decisions with no key and no internet, and give the same answer every time.

This is the difference the whole section is about. Not tidiness — findability, and the ability to change one number and know what it does.

Why this is worth the discipline

The tree is the documentation, and it is the only documentation that cannot go stale.

A README drifts from the code within weeks. Folder names cannot: the build fails if the code stops matching them. Adapters that import the core do not compile. Numbers typed into the core do not compile. Platform words inside the core do not compile. A person joining in a year reads the tree, not a document, and the tree is true by construction.

None of this is extra work bolted onto the schedule. It is the same code, in different folders, with six one-second checks in front of it — and it is the difference between "add Reddit" being a folder and a registry line, versus what adding TikTok actually cost: a second pipeline, a second set of parsing bugs, and a core signal quietly reading zero.

13. How each part gets built

Eleven steps. Each one ships on its own and is useful on its own, and each one ends with a test that either passes or does not — never with somebody deciding it feels finished.

A build plan written as a list of things to make is a wish. The same plan written as a list of things to prove is a schedule. So every step below carries a done when line, and every one of those lines is something a machine can check: a query that returns the right shape, a test that goes red when a threshold moves, a folder whose diff is empty. Nobody has to judge whether the storyteller is good enough. Either the gold set produces a curve or it does not.

The order is not a preference. It comes from one fact: three of the five data streams this business depends on are not being recorded at all today, and a fourth deletes itself every twelve hours. Code can be written later. A week of mint times that were never written down is gone forever. So the steps that start a recorder come before the steps that build anything to look at, even though the recorders produce nothing a person can see.

Three things to notice before the list

The decision log is retrofitted into the pipeline running right now, not the new one. That pipeline makes a few thousand judgements a day and writes down none of them. Every day spent building the replacement first is another day of training data thrown away.

Ten of the eleven steps can be reordered under pressure. This one cannot. Without a stored mint time there is no way to say afterwards whether a call was early or late — so every day it is not running is label data that will never exist, at any price.

14. The timeline, honestly

This is about four months of work, not two. An earlier version of this plan said eight to nine weeks; that number counted the code and nothing else, and it was off by roughly double. 15. Where the line sits

Part four is where the two halves of this document meet. Everything before this was the machine; everything after is the screen. Exactly one rule governs what is allowed to cross between them, and it has already caused one version of this deck to be thrown away.

The rule is short. Facts about the world go on screen. Our reasoning never does. A view count is a fact — the post really has 2.4 million views whether or not Insidor was ever built. A market cap is a fact. So is the minute a post went up, the minute a coin was created, the name of the account that posted it, and how many wallets hold the token. All of those existed before we looked and would carry on existing if we shut the servers down tomorrow.

Our reasoning is a different kind of thing. When the system decides a story is worth showing, it is doing arithmetic: comparing a view count against the same post's view count twenty minutes earlier, weighing how many separate accounts are telling the same ferry-captain story, asking Claude how memeable the thing is and getting a number back. Those numbers are real, and they decide the ordering of the entire feed. But they exist only because we invented them. They are our workings, and workings do not go in front of a user.

In the founder's words: "The app's job is to make the judgement so the user doesn't have to. Showing our workings would just make them do our job for us." That is the whole argument. A trader opening Insidor at 09:13 has perhaps ninety seconds. Hand them a heat score of 8.4 and an acceleration ratio of 3.1x and we have not helped — we have added a second decision on top of the first one and asked them to learn our private scale before they can use the product. The correct output of all that arithmetic is not a number. It is an ordered list and one plain sentence.

One question, applied to every field, plus a second one for anything that survives it. Useful side effect: the numbers that pass are exactly the numbers a sceptical user can verify somewhere else, so showing them buys credibility. The numbers that fail are the ones we could never prove anyway.

There is a discipline hidden in this that is worth naming. A product that never shows its scores cannot hide behind them. If the ordering is wrong, the user sees a bad story at the top and nothing else — no 8.4 to argue with, no gate to blame. The line does not only protect the user from our arithmetic. It removes every excuse for the arithmetic being poor.

16. One story, end to end

One morning, minute by minute, with the machine from Part Three running underneath it. The version of this section in the last deck had the user minting the first coin. The timing measurements killed that. Here is the same morning told honestly: the coins already existed before we had even seen the post, and it made no difference at all to what the user made.

At 09:00 a Norwegian account called @nordkyst_ferge posts forty seconds of video. A retiring ferry captain blows his horn one last time, and every other boat in the harbour blows theirs back. It is a lovely thing and nobody in crypto is looking at it.

Sixty-eight seconds later somebody's bot mints $FERRY. At 09:03 a different bot mints $HORN. By 09:06 there are a dozen coins off that one clip. None of them was made by a person who watched the video and thought it was funny; they were made by software that saw a view count climbing and fired. Our collector does not see the post until 09:04 — by which point two coins are already trading.

Which part of the machine was doing what

At 09:04 the collector did what it does every two minutes: asked our data reseller for posts pulling unusual attention right now. The ferry clip came back at 41,000 views. On its own that number is worthless. It could be a post that exploded in four minutes or one that crawled there over two days, and nothing in the reply tells you which.

So the measurer put it on a list and asked again. At 09:09 the same post read 118,000. That second reading is the whole trick, and it is the one thing on this page nobody will sell us later. A view count is a snapshot anyone can buy; two view counts on the same post at two known times is a fact about the world that only exists if we were standing there writing it down.

The judge — Claude, reading the post, not a model we trained — answered one question at 09:10: could a person plausibly make a coin out of this? Most fast-climbing posts fail that. Politics, sport, a plane crash. A retiring captain and a harbour full of horns passes easily and arrives with its own names attached. At 09:12 the TikTok clip surfaced and the grouper tied the two into one story.

Then the part that is genuinely new. Twelve coins had been minted off this clip, and this is the problem the deck used to skip: a pump.fun search for "chill guy" returns 306 tokens, and only about 5% of coins over $50K declare which post they came from. So the matcher does not read metadata, because there is none. It works from mint time against post time, ticker against the words in the clip, and the shape of the money. It came back with three worth showing and nine not worth the row.

One morning is not evidence

This is a reconstruction of a good day, and it should be read as an illustration of the shape rather than a claim about the hit rate. The honest version of the pitch fits in one line and no longer contains the word first: of the things minted in the last hour, this is the real story, and it is still climbing. That claim survives the timing data. The old one did not.

What we do not yet know is how often a morning like this actually happens — how many of last week’s winners we would have seen at 09:04 rather than 09:47, and how often the coin we labelled biggest was the one that ran. Both questions are answerable, and the only thing standing between us and the answer is that nobody has been writing the mornings down.
